# Xinzuo Blade Steels: Different Balances for Different Knives

Xinzuo uses many blade steels because different knives, users and tasks need different balances of hardness, edge retention, sharpening response, corrosion resistance and toughness. This chapter explains the practical role of each material in the current range.

No steel maximizes every desirable property. A fine slicer, general chef's knife and heavy chopper need different balances. The useful question is not which steel ranks highest, but which steel best matches the knife's geometry, heat treatment, work and maintenance needs.

A steel name describes potential. Heat treatment, blade geometry, thickness behind the edge, sharpening and use determine how that potential appears in the finished knife. [Heat Treatment and Carbides](heat-treatment-and-carbides.md) explains the manufacturing side; this chapter focuses on the practical position of each steel.

## A compact comparison

| Steel | Origin / source | Published hardness | What makes it distinctive in practice | Main compromise or limitation |
|---|---|---:|---|---|
| **ZDP-189** | Japan | 65-67 HRC | Extreme hardness and very high wear resistance give exceptionally long slicing-edge life when geometry and use are appropriate | Demanding sharpening; thin edges require disciplined use and should not be twisted or impacted |
| **SRS13 powder steel** | Japan | 62-64 HRC | Powder-metallurgy structure gives a strong combination of fine cutting response, stainless care and high retention without pushing hardness as far as ZDP-189 | More wear-resistant and less forgiving to sharpen than simpler stainless or carbon steels |
| **Böhler M390** | Austria, Böhler | 60-62 HRC | Premium powder-metallurgy stainless with very high wear resistance and strong corrosion resistance; excellent when long edge life and stainless care matter together | Independent knife-scale testing places its toughness relatively low compared with tougher knife steels; efficient abrasives are useful for sharpening |
| **Blue Steel No. 2** | Japan | 62-64 HRC | Reactive high-carbon steel that can take a very keen edge and respond unusually well to water stones despite high hardness | Requires immediate cleaning and drying and will naturally develop patina |
| **14Cr14MoVNb powder steel** | Xinzuo | 60-64 HRC | Niobium-bearing premium powder steel intended to combine high hardness and retention with a finer, more controlled carbide structure | Premium sharpening requirements; exact behaviour depends strongly on heat treatment and geometry |
| **12Cr18MoV** | Xinzuo | 59-61 HRC | Xinzuo's newest all-round conventional stainless formulation, introduced around 2024 and currently regarded internally as one of the strongest everyday-use balances | Less extreme wear resistance than the hardest powder steels, by design |
| **AUS-10** | Japan, Aichi family | 58-62 HRC | Japanese high-carbon stainless with a practical balance close to the performance class commonly associated with VG10: good sharpness, hardness, corrosion resistance and manageable maintenance | Moderate wear resistance compared with the premium powder-steel tier; not chemically identical to VG10 |
| **10Cr15CoMoV** | China / Xinzuo specification | 58-62 HRC | For years Xinzuo's most widely used high-carbon stainless core because of its unusually complete balance of edge retention, toughness, corrosion resistance and price/performance | Does not maximize one single property; final performance remains highly heat-treatment- and geometry-dependent |
| **VG10** | Japan, Takefu Special Steel | HRC 60 or higher in Takefu's published specification | Benchmark Japanese cobalt-bearing stainless used by many knife makers; combines good corrosion resistance, durable sharpness, wear resistance and comparatively straightforward sharpening | Current Xinzuo procurement is constrained by the producer's China-supply policy; not currently a normal Xinzuo production steel |

| **DIN 1.4116** | Germany | 56-58 HRC | Tough, corrosion-resistant and forgiving in everyday use, with a large margin against chipping and misuse compared with very hard thin-edge steels | Needs more frequent edge maintenance and can form a persistent burr; moderate hardness does not automatically mean effortless sharpening |

This table shows compromises, not rankings. Two steels can feel almost identical in ordinary kitchen use yet separate clearly in a laboratory wear or corrosion test. Two steels at similar hardness can also feel completely different on a sharpening stone.

## Premium powder-metallurgy steels

### ZDP-189: maximum hardness and long edge life

ZDP-189 sits at the extreme-hardness end of the range. At 65-67 HRC, it strongly resists edge deformation and can keep cutting for a long time when geometry and use are appropriate. Its performance comes from hardness and wear resistance together, not HRC alone.

The same properties narrow its margin for misuse. High hardness and a large carbide population slow sharpening, while a thin edge tolerates lateral loads poorly. ZDP-189 is for controlled cutting, not twisting through hard food, levering, striking bone or general abuse.

### SRS13: premium performance without ZDP-189's extreme hardness

SRS13 is specified at 62-64 HRC and used as a high-performance Japanese powder-steel core. Powder metallurgy distributes alloying elements and carbides more evenly than conventional high-alloy ingot processing, supporting a refined edge and strong wear resistance.

SRS13 remains a premium high-retention steel without reaching ZDP-189's hardness. It suits users who want a fine stainless edge and long cutting life without going to the hardest steel in the range. Sharpening still takes more time than with simpler steels, so good abrasives and controlled technique help.

### Böhler M390: wear and corrosion resistance first

M390 is a premium Austrian powder-metallurgy martensitic stainless steel from Böhler. Its main strength is **very high wear resistance combined with strong corrosion resistance**. It suits knives that need long working-edge life with stainless convenience.

Its toughness is more modest. Böhler describes both its toughness and **ductility—the ability to deform before breaking—as good**, while independent knife-scale impact testing places optimized M390 toward the lower-toughness side of the knife-steel spectrum. Earlier unusually high toughness results were linked to heat treatment with excessive retained austenite. This does not make M390 fragile in normal kitchen use; it means its strongest reasons for selection are wear resistance, corrosion resistance and retention rather than exceptional toughness.

Its carbide population also resists ordinary abrasives. Hard ceramic, diamond or **cubic boron nitride (CBN)** abrasives can make sharpening much more efficient. CBN is an extremely hard synthetic abrasive used for difficult-to-abrade steels.

### 14Cr14MoVNb: Xinzuo's niobium-bearing premium powder steel

Xinzuo's 14Cr14MoVNb is a niobium-bearing powder steel used in premium layered constructions. Niobium is a strong carbide former and, in a suitable alloy, can create very hard MC-type carbides while supporting microstructural refinement. The goal is to balance hardness, wear resistance, carbide size and edge stability, not simply add a prestigious element.

The steel is positioned as a premium high-retention option with a broader balance than a maximum-hardness strategy. Selected series use **vacuum heat treatment and deep-freeze processing**, which help determine how much of the alloy's potential reaches the finished blade.

Niobium alone cannot predict performance. Composition and heat treatment still determine the final carbide population.

## Carbon and all-round high-performance stainless steels

### Blue Steel No. 2: high hardness, responsive sharpening

Blue Steel No. 2, or Aogami #2, is the main reactive carbon-steel option in the range and is specified at 62-64 HRC. It reaches high performance through a simpler route than M390 or ZDP-189. Its high-carbon alloy favours a keen, stable edge without relying on a large volume of corrosion-resistant alloy carbides.

A well heat-treated Blue #2 blade can respond very quickly to suitable water stones despite its hardness. It can reach the apex and deburr cleanly, which is why many experienced sharpeners find it more pleasant than lower-HRC stainless steels with greater abrasive wear resistance.

Its main demand is corrosion care. Wash and dry it immediately. Expect a **patina**, the stable grey, blue or dark surface reaction that develops naturally on reactive carbon steel, and distinguish it from active orange rust.

### 10Cr15CoMoV: the long-standing Xinzuo all-round reference

10Cr15CoMoV has long been one of Xinzuo's characteristic steels and, for years, its most widely used high-carbon stainless core. Xinzuo selected it for a broad balance of edge retention, toughness, corrosion resistance, sharpening practicality and cost rather than one extreme property.

That balance gives it an exceptional **quality-to-price ratio**. It can support thin, refined kitchen edges and serious professional use without the material cost or sharpening demands of premium powder steel. For many users, that balance is more useful than a more exotic alloy.

In ordinary kitchen cutting, 10Cr15CoMoV performs very similarly to VG10. Xinzuo's internal comparative testing found a small corrosion-resistance advantage for 10Cr15CoMoV, but the difference is too small to matter for most users. A laboratory can resolve it more easily than a cook can feel it, so the steels belong to broadly comparable everyday performance classes.

Its value lies in its complete, economical balance, not in claiming superiority over VG10 in every category.

### 12Cr18MoV: the newer everyday-use formulation

12Cr18MoV is Xinzuo's newer all-round stainless formulation, used as a core in conventional and copper-Damascus constructions. Current product-development information dates its introduction to around **2024**, making it the newest conventional stainless core steel discussed here.

Its target is practical everyday performance. Chromium, molybdenum and vanadium provide corrosion resistance, stable cutting, useful edge life and adequate toughness without the extreme wear resistance and sharpening difficulty of premium powder steels. Xinzuo currently rates it among its strongest everyday choices because all key daily-use properties remain well balanced.

12Cr18MoV targets different priorities from M390, ZDP-189 or Blue #2. It is designed for frequent use, strong kitchen corrosion resistance, refined cutting and maintenance without specialist abrasives.

### AUS-10: a Japanese all-rounder near the VG10 class

AUS-10 is a Japanese stainless cutlery steel from the Aichi family. Aichi lists it among its higher-carbon knife steels, with molybdenum and vanadium added to improve hardness, wear resistance and tempering resistance. Xinzuo commonly specifies it at 58-62 HRC.

For kitchen use, AUS-10 sits in much the same practical class as **VG10**. They are chemically different, but both are Japanese high-carbon martensitic stainless steels designed to combine good sharpness, useful hardness, corrosion resistance and manageable maintenance.

AUS-10 offers more performance than basic stainless steels while remaining easier to maintain than the highest-wear powder steels. Its strength is balance rather than dominance in one metric.

### VG10: the Japanese benchmark and why it matters to Xinzuo

VG10 is a proprietary Japanese stainless cutlery steel produced by **Takefu Special Steel**. Takefu publishes about 1% carbon, 15% chromium, 1% molybdenum, 0.25% vanadium and roughly 1.55% cobalt, with quenched hardness of HRC 60 or higher. The manufacturer highlights corrosion resistance, durable sharpness, wear resistance, ease of manufacturing and relatively straightforward sharpening.

VG10 is a useful benchmark in premium mass-market Japanese kitchen knives. It helps place AUS-10 and Xinzuo's 10Cr15CoMoV in context: AUS-10 follows a similar all-round stainless philosophy, while 10Cr15CoMoV offers a broadly comparable everyday performance range and showed a small corrosion advantage in Xinzuo's internal testing.

At the time of this edition, **VG10 is not a normal Xinzuo production steel because the producer restricts supply for material destined for China**. Current Xinzuo procurement information indicates that Takefu does not normally authorize VG10 supply for Chinese production. Xinzuo is discussing specific authorization to purchase and use genuine VG10. This may change in future editions and is a procurement issue, not a metallurgical property.

Verified supply matters because Takefu publicly warns about counterfeit or similar products sold under the VG10 name. If Xinzuo adopts it, genuine Takefu material matters more than the letters printed on the blade.

### Sandvik 14C28N: fine structure, nitrogen and edge stability

Sandvik developed 14C28N specifically with knife applications in mind. Nitrogen helps support corrosion resistance while maintaining a fine structure and good edge stability.

14C28N favours a clean, stable edge, strong stainless behaviour and straightforward maintenance rather than M390-level carbide wear resistance. It is especially attractive to users who sharpen their own knives and prefer predictable sharpening to the longest possible interval between sessions.

Its moderate published hardness does not prevent extreme sharpness. Geometry and sharpening create sharpness; steel affects how the edge survives, fails and is restored.

## More forgiving stainless steels

### 440C: the classic high-carbon stainless work steel

440C is a classic high-carbon martensitic stainless steel with a long history in knives, bearings and other wear-resistant components. It provides more carbon and wear resistance than simpler low-carbon stainless grades while retaining good corrosion resistance.

Its carbide structure is generally coarser and its performance ceiling lower than newer powder steels, but it remains a useful working material. At Xinzuo's published 56-58 HRC, it offers useful strength, decent edge life, good stainless care and accessible cost.

Think of it as a proven work steel rather than a prestige steel. It trades some fine-edge retention and high-performance potential for accessibility and predictable service.

### DIN 1.4116: where its forgiveness comes from

Xinzuo specifies DIN 1.4116 at 56-58 HRC in series such as B13S and B35. It emphasizes **corrosion resistance, toughness and forgiving everyday use**. It suits kitchens with less disciplined technique, frequent washing or users who value robustness more than maximum retention.

Moderate hardness can make material removal easier than on a very wear-resistant powder steel, but it does not guarantee easy sharpening. Carbide structure, heat treatment, geometry and burr behaviour also matter. DIN 1.4116 can form a persistent burr and may feel less clean on a water stone than the harder but comparatively fine Blue Steel No. 2.

A better description is **forgiving in use and generally maintainable with ordinary abrasives, but not automatically the easiest steel to apex and deburr**. It usually needs maintenance sooner than harder high-retention steels but offers more margin against chipping and misuse.

Hardness alone is therefore a poor shortcut for sharpening behaviour.

## Layered constructions are not separate “sharpness levels”

Xinzuo also uses layered constructions including conventional Damascus cladding, copper-Damascus designs and 110-layer full Damascus.

Layer count does not directly measure sharpness. In a core-and-cladding blade, the core steel, its heat treatment and edge geometry control the apex. Decorative copper or brass layers can create strong contrast without making the edge inherently sharper.

Full-Damascus constructions differ because alternating materials can continue through the edge and affect wear there. [The Self-Sharpening Effect in Full Damascus Blades](../03-blade-construction/self-sharpening-full-damascus.md) explains this differential wear. It does not mean normal sharpening becomes unnecessary.

## Reading the Xinzuo steel range

The range uses several metallurgical strategies. ZDP-189, SRS13, M390 and 14Cr14MoVNb pursue premium retention through high hardness, powder metallurgy or high-carbide performance, with different trade-offs. Blue Steel No. 2 reaches high hardness through a simpler reactive-carbon route and remains highly responsive on water stones. 10Cr15CoMoV, 12Cr18MoV and AUS-10 occupy the all-round stainless centre, where balance matters more than winning one laboratory category. VG10 provides a useful external Japanese benchmark. 14C28N, 440C and DIN 1.4116 move toward easier care, greater forgiveness and more accessible maintenance.

Do not buy the steel at the top of a table. Choose the Xinzuo knife whose steel, heat treatment, geometry, maintenance needs and sharpening response fit the work and the user.
