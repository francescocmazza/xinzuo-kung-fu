# Xinzuo Blade Steels: Different Metallurgical Balances for Different Knives

Xinzuo uses a broad range of blade steels because different knives, users and cutting tasks demand different balances of hardness, edge retention, sharpening response, corrosion resistance and toughness. This chapter brings those choices together and explains what each material contributes within the current Xinzuo range.

No perfect steel can maximize every desirable property simultaneously. A fine slicer, a general chef's knife and a heavy chopper therefore have good reasons to favour different balances. The value of the range is not that one steel sits at the top of a hierarchy, but that each material can be matched to the geometry, heat treatment, intended work and maintenance expectations of the knife.

A steel name describes potential. The finished knife still depends on heat treatment, blade geometry, thickness behind the edge, sharpening and intended use. The manufacturing side of that relationship is explained in [Heat Treatment and Carbides](heat-treatment-and-carbides.md); here the focus is the practical position each steel occupies in the Xinzuo range.

The table is not a ranking. It is a map of different compromises. Two steels that feel almost equivalent in ordinary kitchen use can still separate clearly in a laboratory corrosion or wear test, while two steels with similar hardness can feel completely different on a sharpening stone.

## Premium powder-metallurgy steels

### ZDP-189: maximum hardness and long edge life

ZDP-189 occupies the extreme-hardness end of the range. At the published 65-67 HRC, it can resist edge deformation very strongly and sustain long cutting life when the edge geometry and cutting task are appropriate. Its attraction is therefore not simply the impressive HRC figure but the combination of hardness and wear resistance that allows a properly sharpened edge to remain effective for a long time.

That performance comes with a narrower operating window. High hardness and a large carbide population make material removal slower during sharpening, while a thin edge in such a hard steel is less tolerant of lateral loads. ZDP-189 is excellent for controlled cutting, not for twisting through hard food, levering, striking bone or using the knife as a general-purpose tool.

### SRS13: premium performance without the extremity of ZDP-189

SRS13 is specified at 62-64 HRC and is used as a high-performance Japanese powder-steel core. Powder metallurgy helps distribute alloying elements and carbides more evenly than a conventional high-alloy ingot route, supporting a refined edge together with strong wear resistance.

In practical terms, SRS13 sits below ZDP-189 in outright hardness but remains clearly in the premium high-retention group. It is a useful choice when the user wants a fine stainless edge and long cutting performance without moving to the most extreme hardness in the range. It is still slower to sharpen than simpler steels, so good stones and controlled technique matter.

### Böhler M390: wear and corrosion resistance first

M390 is a premium Austrian powder-metallurgy martensitic stainless steel developed by Böhler. Its defining strength is the combination of **very high wear resistance with strong corrosion resistance**. That makes it particularly attractive for knives expected to hold a working edge for a long time while retaining the convenience of stainless care.

Its toughness needs a careful explanation. Böhler's industrial data describe M390 toughness and ductility as *good*, but independent knife-scale impact testing paints a more nuanced picture. Earlier unusually high toughness results were traced to a heat-treatment condition with excessive retained austenite; with a more optimized heat treatment, M390 falls on the relatively low-toughness side compared with many tougher knife steels. That does not make it fragile in normal kitchen use, but it does mean that its reputation should rest on wear resistance, corrosion resistance and edge retention rather than on exceptional toughness.

The same carbide population that gives M390 its long wear life also explains why it can feel slow on ordinary abrasives. Hard ceramic, diamond or CBN abrasives can make sharpening substantially more efficient.

### 14Cr14MoVNb: Xinzuo's niobium-bearing premium powder steel

Xinzuo's 14Cr14MoVNb is a niobium-bearing powder steel used in premium layered constructions. Niobium is a strong carbide former; in a carefully designed alloy it can help produce very hard MC-type carbides while supporting microstructural refinement. The purpose is not simply to add another prestigious element to the recipe, but to control how hardness, wear resistance, carbide size and edge stability interact.

Xinzuo positions this steel as a premium high-retention option with a more balanced supporting profile than a simple "maximum hardness" strategy. Selected series using it are documented with **vacuum heat treatment and deep-freeze processing**, which help determine how much of the alloy's potential appears in the finished knife.

The exact final carbide population still depends on composition and heat treatment, so the presence of niobium alone should never be treated as a complete prediction of performance.

## Carbon and all-round high-performance stainless steels

### Blue Steel No. 2: hard does not necessarily mean difficult to sharpen

Blue Steel No. 2, or Aogami #2, is the main reactive carbon-steel option in the range and is specified at 62-64 HRC. It represents a very different route to performance from M390 or ZDP-189: instead of relying on a large volume of corrosion-resistant alloy carbides, it uses a comparatively simple high-carbon alloy designed for a keen, stable cutting edge.

It is particularly useful for correcting a common misconception. Despite its high hardness, a well heat-treated Blue #2 blade can respond very readily to suitable water stones, reach the apex quickly and deburr cleanly. This is why many experienced sharpeners find it more pleasant on a stone than lower-HRC but more wear-resistant stainless steels.

Its principal everyday compromise is corrosion care rather than sharpening effort. Wash and dry it immediately, expect a patina and distinguish that stable dark reaction layer from active orange rust.

### 10Cr15CoMoV: the long-standing Xinzuo all-round reference

10Cr15CoMoV has been one of the most characteristic steels in Xinzuo's range and, for years, the company's most widely used high-carbon stainless core steel. That widespread use was not accidental. Rather than chasing a single extreme property, the alloy was selected for a broad balance of edge retention, toughness, corrosion resistance, sharpening practicality and production cost.

This balance also explains its exceptional **quality-to-price ratio**. It can support thin, refined kitchen edges and serious professional use without imposing the material cost or sharpening demands of a premium powder steel. For many users, that makes the finished knife more useful than a theoretically more exotic steel.

In everyday cutting performance, 10Cr15CoMoV sits very close to VG10. Xinzuo's internal comparative corrosion testing has nevertheless measured a small advantage for 10Cr15CoMoV in corrosion resistance. The difference is real enough to appear under controlled laboratory testing, but it is not large enough to create a meaningful difference for most users in normal kitchen use. In practical daily performance, the two steels should therefore be understood as broadly comparable rather than as belonging to different quality classes.

This distinction is important: a laboratory can resolve differences that a cook may never perceive. The value of 10Cr15CoMoV is not that it "beats" VG10 in every category, but that it delivers a remarkably complete and economical balance.

### 12Cr18MoV: the newer everyday-use formulation

12Cr18MoV is Xinzuo's newer all-round stainless formulation and is used as a core in both conventional Damascus and copper-Damascus constructions. According to current Xinzuo product-development information, the formulation was introduced around **2024**, making it the most recent generation among the company's conventional stainless core steels discussed here.

Its purpose is deliberately practical. Chromium, molybdenum and vanadium are balanced to provide corrosion resistance, stable cutting performance, useful edge life and adequate toughness without pushing the steel into the very high wear resistance and sharpening difficulty of premium powder metallurgy. Within Xinzuo it is currently regarded as one of the company's **top-rated choices for everyday use** because it places all the important daily-use properties in a strong range at the same time.

That description should not be confused with saying that 12Cr18MoV is universally "better" than M390, ZDP-189 or Blue #2. It is optimized around a different target: a knife that can be used frequently, maintained without specialist abrasives, resist normal kitchen corrosion well and still deliver refined cutting performance.

### AUS-10: the Japanese all-rounder close to the VG10 class

AUS-10 is a Japanese stainless cutlery steel from the Aichi steel family. Aichi identifies AUS-10 among its higher-carbon knife steels, with molybdenum and vanadium added to improve hardness, wear resistance and tempering resistance. In Xinzuo knives it is commonly specified at 58-62 HRC.

For a user choosing a kitchen knife, AUS-10 belongs to much the same practical conversation as **VG10**, a steel used extensively by other Japanese and international knife manufacturers. The two are not chemically identical and should not be presented as the same grade, but both are Japanese high-carbon martensitic stainless steels designed to combine good sharpness, useful hardness, corrosion resistance and manageable maintenance.

AUS-10 is therefore an accessible high-quality choice: noticeably more performance-oriented than basic stainless steels, while remaining easier to live with than the highest-wear powder steels. Its value is the absence of a severe weakness rather than dominance in one single metric.

### VG10: the Japanese benchmark and why it matters to Xinzuo

VG10 is a proprietary Japanese stainless cutlery steel produced by **Takefu Special Steel**. Takefu's published composition lists about 1% carbon, 15% chromium, 1% molybdenum, 0.25% vanadium and approximately 1.55% cobalt, with a published quenched hardness of HRC 60 or higher. The manufacturer emphasizes corrosion resistance, long-lasting sharpness, wear resistance, good machinability and relatively straightforward sharpening.

Its importance in this chapter is partly comparative. VG10 has become a familiar reference point in premium mass-market Japanese kitchen knives, so it helps readers understand where AUS-10 and Xinzuo's 10Cr15CoMoV sit. AUS-10 offers a similar all-round Japanese stainless philosophy; 10Cr15CoMoV delivers a broadly comparable everyday performance envelope and, in Xinzuo's internal corrosion testing, a small measurable corrosion-resistance advantage.

At the time of this edition, **VG10 is not a normal Xinzuo production steel because of supply restrictions from the producer for material destined for China**. According to current Xinzuo procurement information, Takefu does not presently authorize normal VG10 supply/export for Chinese production. Xinzuo is in discussion with the producer about obtaining a specific authorization that would allow the company to purchase and use genuine VG10. This is a procurement situation rather than a metallurgical property and may change in future editions.

The distinction between genuine VG10 and look-alike naming is also important. Takefu itself warns publicly about counterfeit or similar products being sold under the VG10 name. If Xinzuo eventually adopts the steel, the relevant point is not merely the letters on the blade but verified supply of genuine Takefu material.

### Sandvik 14C28N: fine structure, nitrogen and edge stability

14C28N was developed by Sandvik specifically with knife applications in mind. Its chemistry uses nitrogen as part of the alloy design, helping support corrosion resistance while maintaining a fine structure and good edge stability.

The practical personality of 14C28N is very different from M390. It does not pursue extremely high carbide wear resistance. Instead, it rewards the user with a clean, stable edge, strong stainless behaviour and comparatively straightforward maintenance. This makes it particularly attractive for people who sharpen their own knives and prefer a steel that responds predictably rather than maximizing the time between sharpening sessions.

A relatively modest published hardness does not prevent a 14C28N knife from being extremely sharp. Sharpness comes from geometry and sharpening; the steel determines how well that edge survives, how it fails and how easily it can be restored.

## More forgiving stainless steels

### 440C: the classic high-carbon stainless work steel

440C is one of the classic high-carbon martensitic stainless steels and has a long history in knives, bearings and other wear-resistant components. It offers more carbon and wear resistance than simpler low-carbon stainless grades while retaining good corrosion resistance.

Compared with newer powder steels, its carbide structure is generally coarser and its performance ceiling is lower, but that does not make it obsolete. At Xinzuo's published 56-58 HRC it gives a robust, understandable working profile: useful strength, decent edge life, good stainless care and a cost structure suitable for dependable everyday knives.

It is therefore best understood as a proven work steel rather than a prestige steel. It sacrifices some fine-edge retention and high-performance potential in exchange for accessibility and predictable service.

### DIN 1.4116: moderate hardness is not the same as easy sharpening

DIN 1.4116 is specified at 56-58 HRC in Xinzuo series such as B13S and B35. Its role in the range emphasizes **corrosion resistance, toughness and forgiving everyday use**. It is especially suitable where the knife may encounter less disciplined technique, frequent washing or users who value robustness more than maximum edge retention.

It should **not** be summarized simply as "easy to sharpen." Moderate hardness can make material removal easier than on a very wear-resistant powder steel, but sharpening response also depends on carbide structure, heat treatment, edge geometry and burr behaviour. 1.4116 can form a persistent burr and may feel less clean on a water stone than a much harder but fine, simple carbon steel such as Blue Steel No. 2.

A better description is therefore: **forgiving to use, generally maintainable with ordinary abrasives, but not automatically the easiest steel to apex and deburr**. It will normally need edge maintenance sooner than the harder high-retention steels, but it offers a larger margin against chipping and misuse.

That distinction is important because it prevents hardness from being used as a shortcut for sharpening behaviour.

## Layered constructions are not separate "sharpness levels"

Xinzuo also uses layered constructions including conventional Damascus cladding, copper-Damascus designs and 110-layer full Damascus.

The number of layers is not a direct measure of sharpness. In a core-and-cladding blade, cutting behaviour is governed principally by the cutting core, heat treatment and edge geometry. Decorative copper or brass layers can create striking contrast without making the apex intrinsically sharper.

Full-Damascus constructions differ because alternating materials can continue through the edge. Their differential-wear behaviour is explained in [The Self-Sharpening Effect in Full Damascus Blades](../03-blade-construction/self-sharpening-full-damascus.md). The term does not mean that ordinary sharpening becomes unnecessary.

## Reading the Xinzuo steel range

The range brings together several different metallurgical strategies. ZDP-189, SRS13, M390 and 14Cr14MoVNb pursue premium retention through high hardness, powder metallurgy or high carbide performance, but they do not all make the same trade-offs. Blue Steel No. 2 reaches high hardness through a much simpler reactive-carbon-steel route and remains remarkably responsive on water stones. 10Cr15CoMoV, 12Cr18MoV and AUS-10 occupy the all-round stainless centre, where balance matters more than winning a single laboratory category. VG10 provides a useful external Japanese benchmark for that same performance class. 14C28N, 440C and DIN 1.4116 move progressively toward easier care, greater forgiveness and more accessible maintenance.

The useful conclusion is not "buy the steel at the top of the table." It is to choose the Xinzuo knife whose steel, heat treatment, geometry, maintenance requirements and sharpening response match the work and the person who will use it.
