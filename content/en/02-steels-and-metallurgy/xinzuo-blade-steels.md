# Xinzuo Blade Steels: Different Metallurgical Balances for Different Knives

Xinzuo uses a broad range of blade steels because different knives, users and cutting tasks require different balances of hardness, edge retention, sharpening response, corrosion resistance and toughness. This chapter brings those choices together and explains what each material contributes within the current Xinzuo range.

No steel can maximize every desirable property at the same time. A fine slicer, a general chef's knife and a heavy chopper therefore have good reasons to use different metallurgical balances. The range becomes useful when each steel is matched to the knife's geometry, heat treatment, intended work and maintenance expectations rather than placed in a single hierarchy.

A steel name describes potential. Heat treatment, blade geometry, thickness behind the edge, sharpening and intended use determine how that potential appears in the finished knife. [Heat Treatment and Carbides](heat-treatment-and-carbides.md) explains the manufacturing side of that relationship; this chapter focuses on the practical position of each steel in the Xinzuo range.

## A compact comparison

The table is a map of different compromises, not a ranking. Two steels can feel almost equivalent in ordinary kitchen use and still separate clearly in a laboratory corrosion or wear test. Conversely, two steels with similar hardness can feel completely different on a sharpening stone.

## Premium powder-metallurgy steels

### ZDP-189: maximum hardness and long edge life

ZDP-189 occupies the extreme-hardness end of the range. At the published 65-67 HRC, it resists edge deformation very strongly and can sustain a long cutting life when the edge geometry and task are appropriate. The combination of hardness and wear resistance allows a properly sharpened edge to remain effective for a long time; the HRC figure alone does not explain that performance.

The same properties narrow the operating window. High hardness and a large carbide population slow material removal during sharpening, and a thin edge in such a hard steel tolerates lateral loads less readily. ZDP-189 is designed for controlled cutting, not for twisting through hard food, levering, striking bone or using the knife as a general-purpose tool.

### SRS13: premium performance without the extremity of ZDP-189

SRS13 is specified at 62-64 HRC and is used as a high-performance Japanese powder-steel core. Powder metallurgy distributes alloying elements and carbides more evenly than a conventional high-alloy ingot route, supporting a refined edge together with strong wear resistance.

SRS13 remains firmly in the premium high-retention group while using less extreme hardness than ZDP-189. It suits a user who wants a fine stainless edge and long cutting performance without moving to the highest hardness in the range. Sharpening still takes more time than it does with simpler steels, so good abrasives and controlled technique matter.

### Böhler M390: wear and corrosion resistance first

M390 is a premium Austrian powder-metallurgy martensitic stainless steel developed by Böhler. Its defining strength is the combination of **very high wear resistance with strong corrosion resistance**. This makes it particularly attractive when a knife is expected to keep a working edge for a long time while retaining the convenience of stainless care.

M390's toughness is more modest than its wear and corrosion performance. Böhler's industrial data describe its toughness and ductility as *good*, while independent knife-scale impact testing places optimized M390 on the relatively low-toughness side of the knife-steel spectrum. Earlier unusually high toughness results were traced to a heat-treatment condition with excessive retained austenite. In normal kitchen use this does not make M390 fragile, but its strongest reasons for selection are wear resistance, corrosion resistance and edge retention rather than exceptional toughness.

The carbide population that gives M390 its long wear life also resists ordinary abrasives. Hard ceramic, diamond or CBN abrasives can therefore make sharpening substantially more efficient.

### 14Cr14MoVNb: Xinzuo's niobium-bearing premium powder steel

Xinzuo's 14Cr14MoVNb is a niobium-bearing powder steel used in premium layered constructions. Niobium is a strong carbide former and, in a carefully designed alloy, can help produce very hard MC-type carbides while supporting microstructural refinement. Xinzuo uses this chemistry to balance hardness, wear resistance, carbide size and edge stability rather than simply adding another prestigious element to the composition.

The steel is positioned as a premium high-retention option with a broader supporting balance than a simple maximum-hardness strategy. Selected series using it are documented with **vacuum heat treatment and deep-freeze processing**, which help determine how much of the alloy's potential appears in the finished knife.

Composition and heat treatment still determine the final carbide population. The presence of niobium alone therefore cannot predict the complete performance of the blade.

## Carbon and all-round high-performance stainless steels

### Blue Steel No. 2: hard does not necessarily mean difficult to sharpen

Blue Steel No. 2, or Aogami #2, is the main reactive carbon-steel option in the range and is specified at 62-64 HRC. It reaches high performance by a very different route from M390 or ZDP-189. Its comparatively simple high-carbon alloy is designed for a keen, stable cutting edge rather than relying on a large volume of corrosion-resistant alloy carbides.

A well heat-treated Blue #2 blade can respond very readily to suitable water stones despite its high hardness. It can reach the apex quickly and deburr cleanly, which is why many experienced sharpeners find it more pleasant on a stone than lower-HRC stainless steels with greater abrasive wear resistance.

Its main everyday demand is corrosion care. Wash and dry the blade immediately, expect a patina and distinguish that stable dark reaction layer from active orange rust.

### 10Cr15CoMoV: the long-standing Xinzuo all-round reference

10Cr15CoMoV has been one of the most characteristic steels in Xinzuo's range and, for years, the company's most widely used high-carbon stainless core steel. Xinzuo selected it for a broad balance of edge retention, toughness, corrosion resistance, sharpening practicality and production cost rather than for one extreme property.

That balance produces an exceptional **quality-to-price ratio**. The steel can support thin, refined kitchen edges and serious professional use without imposing the material cost or sharpening demands of a premium powder steel. For many users, the resulting knife is more useful than one made from a theoretically more exotic alloy.

10Cr15CoMoV performs very similarly to VG10 in ordinary kitchen cutting. Xinzuo's internal comparative testing has measured a small corrosion-resistance advantage for 10Cr15CoMoV, but the difference is too small to create a meaningful separation for most users in normal use. A laboratory can resolve that difference more readily than a cook will perceive it, so the two steels belong to broadly comparable everyday performance classes.

The value of 10Cr15CoMoV lies in this complete and economical balance, not in claiming that it defeats VG10 in every category.

### 12Cr18MoV: the newer everyday-use formulation

12Cr18MoV is Xinzuo's newer all-round stainless formulation and is used as a core in both conventional Damascus and copper-Damascus constructions. Current Xinzuo product-development information dates its introduction to around **2024**, making it the most recent generation among the company's conventional stainless core steels discussed here.

Its design target is deliberately practical. Chromium, molybdenum and vanadium are balanced to provide corrosion resistance, stable cutting performance, useful edge life and adequate toughness without the extreme wear resistance and sharpening difficulty of premium powder metallurgy. Xinzuo currently regards it as one of the company's **top-rated choices for everyday use** because all of the important daily-use properties remain in a strong range at the same time.

12Cr18MoV is optimized for a different target from M390, ZDP-189 or Blue #2 rather than being universally better or worse than those steels. It is intended for frequent use, normal kitchen corrosion resistance, refined cutting performance and maintenance without specialist abrasives.

### AUS-10: the Japanese all-rounder close to the VG10 class

AUS-10 is a Japanese stainless cutlery steel from the Aichi steel family. Aichi identifies AUS-10 among its higher-carbon knife steels, with molybdenum and vanadium added to improve hardness, wear resistance and tempering resistance. In Xinzuo knives it is commonly specified at 58-62 HRC.

For a kitchen-knife buyer, AUS-10 belongs to much the same practical performance class as **VG10**, which is used extensively by other Japanese and international knife manufacturers. The two grades are chemically different, but both are Japanese high-carbon martensitic stainless steels designed to combine good sharpness, useful hardness, corrosion resistance and manageable maintenance.

AUS-10 is therefore an accessible high-quality choice. It delivers more performance than basic stainless steels while remaining easier to maintain than the highest-wear powder steels. Its strength is a well-rounded profile without a severe weakness rather than dominance in one metric.

### VG10: the Japanese benchmark and why it matters to Xinzuo

VG10 is a proprietary Japanese stainless cutlery steel produced by **Takefu Special Steel**. Takefu's published composition lists about 1% carbon, 15% chromium, 1% molybdenum, 0.25% vanadium and approximately 1.55% cobalt, with a published quenched hardness of HRC 60 or higher. The manufacturer emphasizes corrosion resistance, long-lasting sharpness, wear resistance, good machinability and relatively straightforward sharpening.

VG10 is useful here as a familiar benchmark in premium mass-market Japanese kitchen knives. It helps place AUS-10 and Xinzuo's 10Cr15CoMoV in context. AUS-10 follows a similar all-round Japanese stainless philosophy. 10Cr15CoMoV provides a broadly comparable everyday performance envelope and has shown a small measurable corrosion-resistance advantage in Xinzuo's internal testing.

At the time of this edition, **VG10 is not a normal Xinzuo production steel because the producer restricts supply of material destined for China**. Current Xinzuo procurement information indicates that Takefu does not presently authorize normal VG10 supply/export for Chinese production. Xinzuo is discussing a specific authorization that would allow the company to purchase and use genuine VG10. This procurement situation may change in future editions and does not describe a metallurgical property of the steel.

Verified supply matters because Takefu publicly warns about counterfeit or similar products sold under the VG10 name. If Xinzuo adopts the steel, genuine Takefu material is more important than the letters printed on the blade.

### Sandvik 14C28N: fine structure, nitrogen and edge stability

14C28N was developed by Sandvik specifically with knife applications in mind. Its chemistry uses nitrogen as part of the alloy design, helping support corrosion resistance while maintaining a fine structure and good edge stability.

14C28N emphasizes a clean, stable edge, strong stainless behaviour and comparatively straightforward maintenance rather than the extremely high carbide wear resistance of M390. This makes it particularly attractive for people who sharpen their own knives and prefer predictable sharpening response over the longest possible interval between sharpening sessions.

Its relatively modest published hardness does not prevent the finished knife from becoming extremely sharp. Geometry and sharpening create sharpness; the steel influences how well that edge survives, how it fails and how easily it can be restored.

## More forgiving stainless steels

### 440C: the classic high-carbon stainless work steel

440C is one of the classic high-carbon martensitic stainless steels and has a long history in knives, bearings and other wear-resistant components. It provides more carbon and wear resistance than simpler low-carbon stainless grades while retaining good corrosion resistance.

Its carbide structure is generally coarser and its performance ceiling lower than those of newer powder steels, but 440C remains a useful working material. At Xinzuo's published 56-58 HRC it provides a robust and understandable profile: useful strength, decent edge life, good stainless care and a cost structure suited to dependable everyday knives.

It is best understood as a proven work steel rather than a prestige steel. It gives up some fine-edge retention and high-performance potential in exchange for accessibility and predictable service.

### DIN 1.4116: moderate hardness is not the same as easy sharpening

DIN 1.4116 is specified at 56-58 HRC in Xinzuo series such as B13S and B35. Within the range it emphasizes **corrosion resistance, toughness and forgiving everyday use**. It suits kitchens where the knife may encounter less disciplined technique, frequent washing or users who value robustness more than maximum edge retention.

Moderate hardness can make material removal easier than it is on a very wear-resistant powder steel, but it does not automatically make the complete sharpening process easy. Carbide structure, heat treatment, edge geometry and burr behaviour also matter. DIN 1.4116 can form a persistent burr and may feel less clean on a water stone than the much harder but fine and comparatively simple Blue Steel No. 2.

The most useful description is therefore **forgiving to use and generally maintainable with ordinary abrasives, but not automatically the easiest steel to apex and deburr**. It normally needs edge maintenance sooner than the harder high-retention steels and gives the user a larger margin against chipping and misuse.

This distinction prevents hardness from becoming an unreliable shortcut for sharpening behaviour.

## Layered constructions are not separate "sharpness levels"

Xinzuo also uses layered constructions including conventional Damascus cladding, copper-Damascus designs and 110-layer full Damascus.

Layer count does not directly measure sharpness. In a core-and-cladding blade, the cutting core, its heat treatment and the edge geometry govern the behaviour at the apex. Decorative copper or brass layers can create strong visual contrast without making the edge intrinsically sharper.

Full-Damascus constructions differ because alternating materials can continue through the edge and influence wear there. Their differential-wear behaviour is explained in [The Self-Sharpening Effect in Full Damascus Blades](../03-blade-construction/self-sharpening-full-damascus.md). The term does not mean that normal sharpening becomes unnecessary.

## Reading the Xinzuo steel range

The range brings together several metallurgical strategies. ZDP-189, SRS13, M390 and 14Cr14MoVNb pursue premium retention through high hardness, powder metallurgy or high-carbide performance, but each makes different trade-offs. Blue Steel No. 2 reaches high hardness through a much simpler reactive-carbon-steel route and remains remarkably responsive on water stones. 10Cr15CoMoV, 12Cr18MoV and AUS-10 occupy the all-round stainless centre, where balance matters more than winning a single laboratory category. VG10 provides a useful external Japanese benchmark for the same broad performance class. 14C28N, 440C and DIN 1.4116 move progressively toward easier care, greater forgiveness and more accessible maintenance.

The useful conclusion is not to buy the steel at the top of a table. Choose the Xinzuo knife whose steel, heat treatment, geometry, maintenance requirements and sharpening response match the work and the person who will use it.
