# Safe Use and Carrying

Before practising cutting movements, establish the conditions that make those movements predictable. A good kitchen knife cuts with very little effort, so safe use depends less on strength than on **stable food, a stable board, a clear cutting path and consistent habits**.

People often say that a sharp knife is safer than a dull one. The idea is useful but incomplete. A sharp knife usually needs less force, enters food more easily and follows the intended line more predictably; a dull knife often encourages harder, less controlled movement. But a sharp knife can still cause serious injury when the workspace or movement is careless.

Use one short check before every session:

> **Stable board. Stable food. Clear path. Hidden fingertips.**

All four matter. A correct claw grip cannot make a rolling onion safe, and stable food is not enough if the board slides.

## Create the workstation before the cut

Many accidents begin before the blade touches food.

Stabilize the cutting board with a damp cloth, non-slip mat or suitable support if needed. Use a board large enough for the ingredient, the knife and an area for cut pieces. Clear bowls, packaging, utensils and scraps from the path of the blade. Keep the floor and counter dry and use enough light to see the point, the supporting hand and any hard object inside the food.

A useful sequence is:

1. stabilize the board;
2. clear the working area;
3. dry the hands and handle;
4. stabilize the food;
5. choose the correct knife;
6. identify where the blade will travel before beginning.

## Stabilize the ingredient

Round ingredients can roll. Create a flat surface on an onion, potato, pumpkin or melon before making the main cuts, then place that surface on the board.

Divide large or irregular food into manageable sections. Forcing a long knife through something that cannot be held securely increases the chance of slipping, twisting or releasing suddenly.

Avoid frozen food unless the knife is specifically designed for it. A hard frozen surface can make a fine edge skid, chip or break through unpredictably.

## Use the right knife for the task

Kitchen knives are not universal cutting tools. A thin gyuto, santoku or nakiri is excellent for food preparation but not heavy bone. A flexible fillet knife follows contours but should not be twisted against a hard joint. A deba can handle appropriate fish-butchery work but is not a universal cleaver.

Do not use a kitchen knife as a pry bar, screwdriver, can opener, lever, shell-opening tool or tool for separating frozen objects. Do not use the cutting edge as a sideways scraper.

The steel and geometry are designed for cutting. A load that is safe in the intended cutting direction can become damaging when the blade is twisted or levered sideways.

## Grip and supporting hand

Safety does not depend on one universal knife-hand grip. The **handle grip, intermediate or hybrid grip, and pinch grip** described in [Weight, Balance and Ergonomics](../05-knife-types/weight-and-balance.md) can all be controlled when they suit the knife and task.

Whatever grip you use, hold the knife securely without unnecessary tension, keep fingers from sliding toward the edge and keep the wrist in a natural line. If a new grip changes the balance you feel, slow down until the knife becomes predictable again.

The supporting hand guides the food while keeping fingertips outside the cutting path. In the **claw grip**, fingertips curl inward and the side of the blade can move against the first joints. The claw is a moving guide, not a pose to imitate at professional speed.

The next chapter explains the cutting movements themselves. At this stage the rule is simpler: **set the supporting hand first, then move the knife**.

## Use only the force the cut requires

A well-sharpened knife should not need excessive force. If the blade does not enter easily, stop and identify the reason. The edge may be dull, the food unstable, the knife unsuitable or the movement wrong for the profile.

Excessive force can make the knife slip, make the food move suddenly, wedge the blade and release it unpredictably, or encourage the user to twist the blade while freeing it. Use smooth, deliberate movement and avoid lateral stress.

## Use a suitable cutting board

For safety, the board must do two things: **remain stable under the food and provide a cutting surface suitable for a knife edge**.

Wood and purpose-made plastic or resilient synthetic boards can provide appropriate cutting surfaces when they are clean, flat and in good condition. Glass, ceramic, stone and metal are too hard for routine cutting with fine edges and can rapidly damage the apex.

The detailed differences between wood species, **end grain**, edge grain, plastic, resilient rubber, bamboo and composite boards belong in the dedicated [Cutting Boards](../09-maintenance/cutting-boards.md) chapter. Here, do not let board choice distract from the immediate safety rule: the board must not slide, and the cutting surface must not force the knife to rebound or lose its edge unnecessarily.

## Avoid twisting and sideways scraping

This matters especially with high-hardness, thin-edged knives. A precision edge can be extremely strong in its intended cutting direction and much less tolerant of sideways force.

If a blade becomes stuck in dense food, withdraw it carefully and adjust the cut instead of twisting it free.

Do not scrape food across the board with the cutting edge. Use the spine, blade face or a bench scraper instead.

## Wash safely as well as carefully

[Routine Knife Care](../09-maintenance/routine-care.md) explains material care. During washing, the immediate safety rule is simple: **keep the blade visible and its edge path controlled**.

Never leave a knife hidden under soapy water or mixed with other objects in the sink. Wash it individually with mild detergent and a soft sponge, keeping the edge away from the hand. Dry it immediately from the spine side without wrapping fingers around the cutting edge.

A dishwasher can also hide a sharp blade among other utensils. Hand washing avoids both the safety and material-care problems.

## Passing a knife to another person

Passing a knife should be a deliberate, almost ritual movement. Do not hand it over as if it were an ordinary object.

Open both hands with the palms facing upward and lay the knife flat across them instead of gripping it. Support the handle with one palm and the broad, flat side of the blade with the other. Keep the cutting edge completely clear of the fingers and palms, with the handle oriented toward the person receiving the knife. Hold the hands still and present the whole knife visibly.

The other person takes the knife by the handle and lifts it from the open palms. The person offering the knife does not close the fingers around it, pull it back or make another movement while it is being taken. The transfer is complete only when the other person has full control of the handle.

This makes the transfer clear and predictable: the knife is visible, only one person controls the handle at a time, and the person receiving it decides when to take it.

Never throw, slide or casually reach a knife toward someone.

## Moving with a knife in the kitchen

For short distances, keep the knife low, close to the body and still.

1. Hold the handle firmly.
2. Keep the arm close to your side.
3. Point the tip toward the floor.
4. Keep the edge facing backward or otherwise away from the body.
5. Do not swing the arm.
6. Warn nearby people that you are passing with a knife.
7. Walk slowly; never run.

Pointing the tip down reduces the chance of directing it toward another person's upper body. Keeping the arm still makes your movement easier for other people to predict.

Do not cover the tip with the free hand or carry the knife upright near the face or chest. In a professional kitchen, use a clear warning such as “knife behind” or the workplace's established expression.

## Transport beyond the workstation

Do not carry a knife loose through a building, place it unprotected in a bag or leave it in a drawer with the edge exposed.

Use a blade guard, saya, fitted case, knife roll or secure packaging designed for transport. The protection must prevent the knife from sliding out and the edge from cutting through the material.

For transport outside the kitchen, follow local laws and venue rules.

## Safe storage

Store knives with the edge protected and the handle accessible without touching the blade. Suitable options include a well-designed knife block, a stable magnetic strip, an in-drawer organizer, a blade guard or saya, and a knife roll for professional transport.

A loose knife in a drawer is unsafe and can damage the edge against other utensils.

On a magnetic strip, place the spine or blade face against the strip first and rotate the blade into position under control. To remove it, rotate the edge away before pulling. This avoids striking or scraping the edge against the holder.

## What to do if a knife falls

Never try to catch a falling knife.

Move your feet away and let the knife fall. Once it stops moving, pick it up by the handle and inspect it for damage.

## Sharpness and safety

Sharpen a dull knife instead of compensating with force. Signs that the edge needs attention include sliding on tomato skin, crushing herbs instead of cutting cleanly, needing excessive pressure to begin a cut, tearing paper or food, and moving unpredictably across the ingredient.

Sharpness improves control, but it does not replace safe technique.

## Common unsafe habits

### Leaving a knife in the sink

A hidden blade can cut anyone reaching into the water. Keep the knife visible until it is washed, dried and stored.

### Cutting toward the body

Arrange the board, ingredient and stance so the projected cutting path remains clear of hands and body. Specialist in-hand work uses a different, deliberately controlled setup and should be learned separately.

### Holding food in the air with a large knife

Use a cutting board unless the task specifically calls for controlled in-hand work with a suitable small knife. A large blade and unsupported food create unnecessary risk.

### Testing sharpness with a finger

Never use a finger to test whether a knife is sharp, either by sliding along the edge or by pressing against it. Test sharpness only on suitable food or paper. The only tactile edge check taught in this guide is the controlled **burr check during sharpening**: the finger moves from the blade face toward the edge, perpendicular to it, and stops at the apex.

### Scraping the board with the cutting edge

Use the spine, blade face or a bench scraper. Sideways scraping damages the apex and creates unnecessary lateral movement near the guiding hand.

### Using a glass, ceramic, stone or metal board

These surfaces rapidly damage fine edges and can make cutting less predictable. Use a suitable cutting board designed for knife work.

## What to remember

> A sharp knife is easier to control because it needs less force, but safety depends on the whole working method. Stabilize the board and food, use the correct knife, keep the supporting hand clear, avoid twisting and make every movement predictable to the people around you.

The safest cook is not the fastest. It is the one whose movements remain controlled and easy to anticipate.

With that framework established, the next chapter can focus on **how to move the knife**: direct descent, advancing cuts, draw slicing, pivoted work and precise tip control.

> **Practice principle:** make every knife movement easy for another person to anticipate.
