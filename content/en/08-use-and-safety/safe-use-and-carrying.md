# Safe Use and Carrying

A good kitchen knife cuts with very little effort. That is why safety must come from habits, not hesitation.

People often say a sharp knife is safer than a dull one. The idea is useful but incomplete.

A sharp knife usually needs less force, enters food more easily and follows the intended line more predictably. A dull knife often encourages harder, less controlled movement. But a sharp knife can still cause serious injury if handled carelessly.

Safety comes from a suitable sharp knife, a stable workspace, controlled technique and clear habits.

Use one short check before every session:

> **Stable board. Stable food. Clear path. Hidden fingertips.**

All four matter. A perfect claw grip cannot rescue a rolling onion, and stable food is not enough if the board slides.

## Create a safe working area before cutting

Many accidents begin before the blade touches food.

Stabilize the cutting board with a damp cloth, non-slip mat or suitable support if needed. Use a board large enough for both the ingredient and the knife. A board that is too small forces food, blade and hands into the same limited space.

Keep the work area clear. Bowls, packaging, utensils and scraps should not block the cutting path. Keep the floor and counter dry, and use enough light to see the tip, supporting hand and any hard objects inside the food.

A simple routine is:

1. Stabilize the board.
2. Clear the working area.
3. Dry the hands and handle.
4. Stabilize the food.
5. Choose the correct knife.

These few seconds reduce risk and improve cutting quality.

## Stabilize the ingredient

Round ingredients can roll. Create a flat surface on an onion, potato, pumpkin or melon before making the main cuts, then place that surface on the board.

Divide large or irregular food into manageable sections. Forcing a long knife through something that cannot be held securely increases the risk of slipping or twisting.

Avoid frozen food unless the knife is specifically designed for it. A hard frozen surface can make the edge skid, chip or break through suddenly with uncontrolled force.

## Use the right knife for the task

Kitchen knives are not universal cutting tools.

A thin gyuto, santoku or nakiri is excellent for food preparation but not heavy bone. A flexible fillet knife follows contours but should not be twisted against a hard joint. A deba can handle appropriate fish bones but is not a universal cleaver.

Do not use a kitchen knife as:

- a pry bar;
- a screwdriver;
- a can opener;
- a tool for separating frozen objects;
- a lever for opening shells or lids;
- a scraper with the cutting edge dragged sideways across the board.

The steel and geometry are designed for cutting. Sideways force can damage an edge even when straight cutting force is safe.

## Grip and control

Safety does not depend on one universal knife-hand grip. The **handle grip, intermediate or hybrid grip, and pinch grip** described in [Weight and Balance](../05-knife-types/weight-and-balance.md) can all be controlled when they suit the knife and task.

Whatever grip you use, hold the knife securely without unnecessary tension, keep fingers from sliding toward the edge and keep the wrist in a natural line. If a new grip changes the balance you feel, slow down until the knife becomes predictable again.

The supporting hand should guide the food while keeping fingertips out of the cutting path. In the **claw** grip, fingertips curl inward and the side of the blade moves against the first joints. The claw is a moving guide, not a pose to imitate at professional speed.

[How to Cut](../06-cutting-techniques/essential-cutting-techniques.md) explains the movements themselves. The safety rule is simpler: use a stable grip, keep the supporting hand behind the cutting line and never trade control for speed.

## Let the edge do the work

A well-sharpened knife should not need excessive force.

If the blade does not enter easily, stop and find the reason. The edge may be dull, the food unstable, the knife unsuitable or the movement wrong for the profile.

Excessive force can make:

- the knife slip;
- the food move suddenly;
- the edge wedge and release unpredictably;
- the user twist the blade while freeing it;
- the supporting hand move into the path.

Use smooth, deliberate movement and avoid lateral stress.

## Use a suitable cutting board

Wood and suitable plastic boards are generally gentle enough for quality knife edges.

Glass, ceramic, stone and metal are too hard for fine edges. Repeated contact can dull or chip the apex quickly. Decorative glass boards may be easy to clean, but they are poor cutting surfaces.

Maintain the board too. Deep grooves can trap residue and affect stability. Clean and dry wooden boards properly instead of soaking them.

## Avoid twisting and sideways scraping

This matters especially with Xinzuo's higher-hardness steels. ZDP-189, SRS13, M390, Blue Steel No. 2 and 14Cr14MoVNb are chosen for demanding edge performance, but a thin hard edge is not designed to pry, twist through a cut, strike bone or cut frozen food. More forgiving steels and heavier geometries offer a larger margin, but they still do not turn a chef's knife into a cleaver or pry tool.

A thin knife is strong in its intended cutting direction and much less tolerant of sideways force.

If a blade becomes stuck in dense food, withdraw it carefully and adjust the cut instead of twisting it free.

Do not scrape food across the board with the cutting edge. Use the spine, blade face or a bench scraper instead.

## Wash safely as well as carefully

[Routine Knife Care](../09-maintenance/routine-care.md) explains material care. During washing, the safety rule is simple: **keep the blade visible and its edge path controlled**.

Never leave a knife hidden under soapy water or mixed with other objects in the sink. Wash it individually with mild detergent and a soft sponge, keeping the edge away from the hand. Dry it immediately from the spine side without wrapping fingers around the edge.

A dishwasher can also hide a sharp blade among other utensils. Hand washing solves both the safety and material-care problems.

## Passing a knife to another person

Whenever possible, do not pass a knife directly hand to hand.

Place it on a clear surface with the handle accessible and tell the other person it is there. If a direct handover is unavoidable, communicate clearly, move slowly and offer the handle while keeping the blade controlled and pointed away from both people.

Never throw, slide or casually reach a knife toward someone.

## Moving with a knife in the kitchen

For short distances, keep the knife low, close to the body and still.

1. Hold the handle firmly.
2. Keep the arm close to your side.
3. Point the tip toward the floor.
4. Keep the edge facing backward or otherwise away from the body.
5. Do not swing the arm.
6. Warn nearby people that you are passing with a knife.
7. Walk slowly; never run.

Pointing the tip down reduces the chance of directing it toward another person's upper body. Keeping the edge away and the arm still reduces accidental contact.

Do not cover the tip with the free hand or carry the knife upright near the face or chest.

In a professional kitchen, use a clear warning such as “knife behind” or the workplace's established expression.

## Transport beyond the workstation

Do not carry a knife loose through a building, place it unprotected in a bag or leave it in a drawer with the edge exposed.

Use:

- a blade guard;
- a saya;
- a fitted case;
- a knife roll;
- secure packaging designed for transport.

The protection must prevent the knife from sliding out and the edge from cutting through the material.

For transport outside the kitchen, follow local laws and venue rules.

## Safe storage

Store knives with the edge protected and the handle accessible without touching the blade.

Suitable options include:

- a well-designed knife block;
- a stable magnetic strip installed correctly;
- an in-drawer organizer;
- a blade guard or saya;
- a knife roll for professional transport.

A loose knife in a drawer is unsafe and also damages the edge against other utensils.

On a magnetic strip, place the spine against the strip first and rotate the blade into position. To remove it, rotate the edge away before pulling. This avoids striking or scraping the edge against the magnet.

## What to do if a knife falls

Do not try to catch it.

Move your feet away and let the knife fall. Once it stops moving, pick it up by the handle and inspect it for damage.

## Sharpness and safety

Sharpen a dull knife instead of compensating with force.

Signs that the edge needs attention include:

- sliding on tomato skin;
- crushing herbs instead of cutting cleanly;
- needing pressure to begin a cut;
- tearing paper or food;
- moving unpredictably across the ingredient.

Sharpness improves control, but it does not replace safe technique.

## Common unsafe habits

### Leaving a knife in the sink

A hidden blade can cut anyone reaching into the water. Keep the knife visible until it is washed, dried and stored.

### Cutting toward the body

Direct cuts away from hands and body whenever possible. If specialist in-hand work is appropriate, use a small suitable knife and keep the movement short and controlled.

### Holding food in the air with a large knife

Use a cutting board unless the task specifically calls for controlled in-hand work with a suitable small knife. A large blade and unsupported food create unnecessary risk.

### Testing sharpness by sliding a finger along the edge

Never slide a finger lengthwise along the cutting edge. Use visual inspection or a controlled cutting test instead.

### Scraping the board with the cutting edge

Use the spine, blade face or a bench scraper. Sideways scraping damages the apex and creates unnecessary lateral movement near the guiding hand.

### Using a glass, ceramic, stone or metal board

These surfaces rapidly damage fine edges and can make cutting less predictable. Use wood or suitable plastic.

### Trying to catch a falling knife

Step back and let it fall. A damaged knife can be repaired or replaced; a hand injury is more serious.

## What to remember

> A sharp knife is easier to control because it needs less force, but safety depends on the whole working method. Stabilize the board and food, use the correct knife, keep the supporting hand clear, avoid twisting and carry the knife low and still with the tip down and the edge away.

The safest cook is not the fastest. It is the one whose movements remain predictable.

> **Practice principle:** make every knife movement easy for another person to anticipate.
