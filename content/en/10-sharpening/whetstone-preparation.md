# Preparing Water Stones

A sharpening stone is a simple tool, but preparation changes how it cuts, feels and wears.

Water fills the pores of absorbent stones, carries away steel particles, helps control friction and supports abrasive action.

A useful general rule is:

> Most traditional absorbent water stones should be soaked until air bubbles have largely stopped, unless the manufacturer says otherwise.

This follows the stone's behaviour instead of a fixed number of minutes.

## Why bubbles appear

A dry porous stone contains air. When placed in water, the pores fill and push that air out as bubbles.

Bubbling is usually strongest at first and slows as the stone becomes saturated. When only occasional bubbles remain, the stone has usually absorbed enough water for sharpening.

Time varies with porosity, thickness, dryness, temperature, size and **binder**, the material that holds the abrasive grains together. For absorbent stones, watching the bubbles is often more useful than always soaking for exactly ten minutes.

## The important exception: follow the manufacturer

Not every sharpening stone should be soaked.

Modern stones use different abrasives and binders. Some absorb water; others need only a wet surface. Long immersion can weaken certain constructions.

The manufacturer's instructions take priority because they match the actual stone.

A stone may require:

- soaking until bubbling stops;
- a short fixed soak;
- a splash of water;
- no soaking;
- oil or another lubricant instead of water.

Dense ceramic and splash-and-go stones may need only surface wetting. Some resin-bonded stones can soften, crack, separate or lose strength if soaked too long. Diamond plates do not absorb water.

The rule is not “all stones must soak.” Saturate absorbent water stones properly and follow the instructions for every other type.

## Types of sharpening media

Xinzuo's broad steel range is one reason no single stone suits every knife. Conventional water stones work well with many DIN 1.4116, 440C, 14C28N, AUS-10, 10Cr15CoMoV, 12Cr18MoV and Blue Steel No. 2 edges. Hard ceramic or diamond abrasives may work more efficiently on the greater wear resistance of ZDP-189, SRS13, M390 and 14Cr14MoVNb. Preparation still follows the stone maker's instructions, not the steel name.

### Absorbent water stones

These porous stones take in water and normally benefit from immersion before use. Water keeps the surface active and prevents it from drying during sharpening.

### Splash-and-go water stones

These dense stones work after water is applied to the surface. They need little preparation time, and long soaking can damage some models.

### Ceramic stones

*Ceramic* covers several constructions. Some are porous and need a short soak; others are dense and splash-and-go. Follow the product instructions.

### Diamond plates

Diamond abrasive is fixed to a rigid plate. The plate does not need soaking. Water may help move loose steel residue and reduce clogging, but it is not absorbed.

### Oil stones

Oil stones use honing oil as lubricant. Do not casually switch porous stones between oil and water. Once oil penetrates a stone, returning to water may be difficult.

### Natural stones

Natural stones vary widely. Some absorb water, some need only surface wetting and some can be damaged by long soaking. Follow guidance specific to the stone.

Their behaviour also shows why grit alone does not describe a stone. Porosity, mineral structure, cutting speed and abrasive release can make stones used at similar finishing stages feel very different.

## Preparing the workspace

Use a stable surface at a comfortable height. Repeated sharpening strokes require controlled body position, not an awkward bend over a low table.

Secure the stone in a holder, on a non-slip base or on a damp cloth. A moving stone makes angle control difficult and can be dangerous.

## Flatness: diagnose before correcting

A stone wears where the knife contacts it most. The centre can gradually become hollow while the ends stay high.

A dished stone changes the angle during the stroke. On a wide single bevel, it can alter the geometry significantly.

Check the stone in more than one direction. A reliable straight edge can reveal a hollow through the centre; reflected light under the straight edge makes small gaps easier to see. A few light pencil witness lines across different parts of the surface can provide a second check: high areas lose the marks first, while low areas retain them.

Correct the surface with a flattening plate, lapping stone or other flat abrasive recommended for that stone. Use strokes that cover the whole face and rotate the stone during the work so hand pressure does not create a new bias. Rinse away loose abrasive, check again across both diagonals and the length, and continue only where a gap or witness mark remains.

The target is not merely a clean-looking surface. It is one plane that supports a repeatable angle across the full sharpening stroke. Check often enough that flattening remains light maintenance rather than a major correction.

## Wetting the surface before sharpening

Even a soaked stone needs water on its working surface.

Keep the surface wet and free-moving. Add water if it begins to feel sticky or dry.

The right amount depends on the stone. Too little can let removed steel build up and clog or **glaze** the surface, making it smoother and less effective; too much may wash away useful abrasive mixture too quickly. A dry grinding sound, sudden increase in resistance or dark dry streaks often mean more water is needed.

## Slurry and swarf

Sharpening often creates a grey or dark mixture containing:

- water;
- steel removed from the blade;
- abrasive released from the stone;
- binder particles.

The loose abrasive-and-water mixture is often called **slurry**. Removed material, especially steel, is often called **swarf**.

This dark residue is not the burr. The burr remains attached to the edge until removed.

Whether to keep or rinse slurry depends on the stone and sharpening stage. On some stones it adds useful abrasive action and smooths the finish. On others, too much swarf clogs the surface and slows cutting. Coarse repair may benefit from rinsing; fine polishing may benefit from controlled slurry.

There is no universal rule that slurry must always stay or always be removed.

## Keeping the stone wet during use

Absorbent stones can dry during long sessions. Add water before the surface becomes completely dry.

Keep enough water to:

- move the blade smoothly;
- carry away loose steel;
- reduce loading;
- maintain consistent abrasive action.

Splash-and-go stones still need surface water even though they are not soaked.

## Soaking several stones

When using several stones, check which ones can safely share a water container.

Coarse stones release abrasive into the water. A fine finishing stone can pick up those particles and produce scratches. Use clean water and rinse stones before moving to finer stages. Very fine or delicate stones may deserve a separate container.

Do not leave a stone permanently submerged unless the manufacturer explicitly allows it.

## After sharpening

Rinse away loose steel and abrasive residue. Clean the surface gently and treat glazed areas with the appropriate cleaning or flattening method.

Check flatness; it is often easiest to flatten the stone while it is already wet.

Let it dry fully in a ventilated place. Avoid:

- direct heat;
- radiators;
- ovens;
- strong sunlight;
- freezing while the stone contains water;
- closed containers while it is damp.

Rapid drying can cause cracks. Closed storage while wet can create mould and odours. A thick stone may feel dry outside while still holding moisture inside, so give it enough time before storage.

## Common mistakes

### Soaking every stone overnight

Some stones tolerate it; others are damaged. Follow the manufacturer.

### Using a fixed time without observing the stone

A fixed five or ten minutes may be right for one stone and wrong for another. Bubbles are a useful guide for absorbent stones.

### Sharpening on a dry surface

The stone can load, drag and cut inconsistently.

### Ignoring flatness

A hollow stone changes the sharpening angle and makes results harder to repeat.

### Drying with heat

Rapid drying can crack the stone.

### Storing the stone wet

Trapped moisture can cause mould, odour and damage.

### Confusing slurry with the burr

Slurry is loose material on the stone. The burr is a thin lip of steel attached to the edge.

## Three preparation checks

**Identify the stone.** Read its instructions and determine whether it needs soaking, surface water, oil or no liquid. Inspect it for cracks, contamination and uneven wear.

**Make the platform reliable.** Correct flatness where necessary, place the stone on a non-slip support and leave enough room for the complete stroke. Wet or lubricate the working face as specified.

**Plan the end of the session.** Keep clean water or the correct lubricant nearby. After sharpening, remove residue, recheck the surface and let the stone dry as its construction requires.

Preparation should respond to the stone's **actual construction**. The aim is a stable, flat and correctly conditioned abrasive surface, not the mechanical repetition of one ritual for every product.
