# Preparing Water Stones

A sharpening stone looks simple: a flat block that removes steel. Yet the way the stone is prepared has a direct effect on how it cuts, how it feels and how long it lasts.

Water does more than make the surface slippery. It enters the pores of absorbent stones, carries away steel particles, helps control friction and supports the abrasive action at the surface.

The most useful general rule is:

> Most traditional absorbent water stones should be soaked until the release of air bubbles has substantially stopped, unless the manufacturer gives different instructions.

This rule responds to the behaviour of the stone instead of relying on one fixed number of minutes.

## Why bubbles appear

A dry porous stone contains air in its internal spaces. When the stone is placed in water, water moves into those spaces and pushes the air out. The air rises as bubbles.

The stream may be active at first. It slows as the stone becomes saturated. When only an occasional bubble remains, the stone has usually absorbed the water it needs for sharpening.

The exact time varies. A thick, dry and very porous stone may need longer than a dense or partially damp one. Temperature, stone size and binder also influence absorption.

“Until the bubbles substantially stop” is therefore often more practical than “always soak for exactly ten minutes.”

## The important exception: follow the manufacturer

Not every sharpening stone should be soaked.

Modern stones use different abrasives and binders. Some are designed to absorb water. Others are dense and need only a wet surface. Long immersion can weaken some constructions.

The manufacturer's instructions always take priority because they reflect the stone's construction.

A stone may require:

- soaking until bubbling stops;
- a fixed short soak;
- a quick splash of water;
- no soaking at all;
- oil or another lubricant instead of water.

Dense ceramic and splash-and-go stones are common examples that may need only surface wetting. Resin-bonded stones can soften, crack, separate or lose strength if left in water too long. Diamond plates do not absorb water and should not be treated like porous stones.

The correct lesson is not "all stones must soak." Soak an absorbent water stone until it is properly saturated, while following the manufacturer's instructions for the particular product.

## Types of sharpening media

Xinzuo's broad steel range is one reason no single stone should be prescribed for every knife. Conventional water stones can be effective for many DIN 1.4116, 440C, 14C28N, AUS-10, 10Cr15CoMoV, 12Cr18MoV and Blue Steel No. 2 edges. Hard ceramic or diamond abrasives may work more efficiently on the higher wear resistance of ZDP-189, SRS13, M390 and 14Cr14MoVNb. Stone preparation still follows the stone maker's instructions, not the Xinzuo steel name.

Understanding the main families helps explain why preparation differs.

### Absorbent water stones

These stones contain pores that take in water. They normally benefit from immersion before use. The water keeps the surface active and prevents the stone from drying during sharpening.

### Splash-and-go water stones

These are dense stones designed to work after water is applied to the surface. They are convenient because there is little waiting time. Long soaking may damage some models.

### Ceramic stones

The word ceramic covers several constructions. Some ceramic stones are porous and need a short soak. Others are dense and splash-and-go. The label alone is not enough; use the product instructions.

### Diamond plates

A diamond plate has abrasive particles fixed to a rigid surface. It does not need soaking. Water may be used to move swarf and reduce loading, but the plate does not absorb it.

### Oil stones

Oil stones are designed to use honing oil as a lubricant. They can sharpen kitchen knives, but water and oil should not be casually exchanged. Once a porous stone has absorbed oil, changing back to water may be difficult.

### Natural stones

Natural stones vary widely. Some absorb water, some need only surface wetting and some can be damaged by prolonged soaking. Follow guidance from the seller or an experienced user familiar with that particular stone.

Their behaviour also shows why a grit description cannot tell the whole story. Porosity, mineral structure, cutting speed and the way a natural stone releases abrasive material can make two stones used at a similar finishing stage feel very different in practice.

## Preparing the workspace

Stone preparation includes more than soaking.

Choose a stable work surface at a comfortable height. Sharpening requires repeated controlled movement, so the body should not be bent awkwardly over a low table.

Place the stone in a holder, on a non-slip base or on a damp cloth that prevents movement. A stone that slides during sharpening makes angle control difficult and can be dangerous.

Keep water nearby. A small container or squeeze bottle makes it easy to wet the surface without interrupting the process.

Arrange the workspace so the knife can move through the full stroke without hitting a wall, tap, bowl or other object.

Before beginning, check that the stone is clean and reasonably flat.

## Flatness: why the stone's shape matters

A sharpening stone gradually wears where the knife contacts it most. Over time, the centre may become hollow while the ends remain high.

A dished stone makes consistent sharpening difficult. The blade follows the curved surface, and the angle changes during the stroke. On a wide single bevel, an uneven stone can alter the geometry significantly.

A simple way to check flatness is to place a straight edge across the surface or draw a pencil grid over the stone. If the grid disappears unevenly during flattening, the surface was not flat.

Use a dedicated flattening stone, lapping plate or suitable abrasive surface according to the manufacturer's guidance. Rub evenly until the pencil marks have disappeared.

Flattening is not a rare repair. It is part of regular stone maintenance.

## Wetting the surface before sharpening

Even after soaking, add water to the working surface before placing the knife on it.

The surface should look wet and feel free-moving. Add more water if the stone begins to feel sticky or dry.

The correct amount varies. Too little water can allow swarf to build up and make the stone feel glazed. Excessive water may wash away slurry faster than desired on some stones. The user learns to adjust by observing the sound and feel.

A dry grinding sound, sudden increase in resistance or dark dry streaks are signs that more water may be needed.

## Slurry and swarf

During sharpening, the surface often develops a grey or dark mixture.

This mixture may contain:

- water;
- steel removed from the blade;
- abrasive particles released from the stone;
- binder particles.

The loose abrasive-and-water mixture is often called **slurry**. Removed material, especially steel particles, is often called **swarf**.

The dark colour is not the burr. It is residue on the stone. The burr remains attached to the edge until it is removed.

Whether slurry should be kept or rinsed away depends on the stone and stage of sharpening.

On some stones, slurry contributes useful abrasive action and creates a smooth finish. On others, excessive swarf clogs the surface and slows cutting. Rinsing can keep the stone aggressive during coarse repair, while controlled slurry may help during fine polishing.

There is no universal rule that slurry must always remain or always be removed.

## Keeping the stone wet during use

An absorbent stone can begin to dry during a long sharpening session.

Add water regularly. Do not wait until the surface is completely dry.

The stone should remain wet enough to:

- move the blade smoothly;
- carry away loose steel;
- prevent excessive loading;
- maintain consistent abrasive action.

A splash-and-go stone still needs surface water even though it was not soaked.

## Soaking several stones

When using a progression, identify which stones may safely share the water container.

A coarse stone can release loose abrasive into the water. A fine finishing stone soaked in the same dirty container can pick up coarse particles that create scratches.

Use clean water and rinse the stones before moving to a finer stage. Very fine or delicate stones may deserve a separate container.

Never leave a stone permanently submerged unless the manufacturer explicitly permits it. Some traditional stones tolerate long-term soaking; others deteriorate.

## After sharpening

Stone care continues after the knife is finished.

Rinse away loose steel and abrasive residue. Clean the surface gently according to the stone's needs. If the stone has developed dark glazed areas, use an appropriate dressing or flattening method.

Check flatness. It is often easier to flatten the stone immediately after use while it is already wet.

Allow the stone to dry fully in a ventilated place.

Avoid:

- direct heat;
- radiators;
- ovens;
- strong sunlight;
- freezing conditions while the stone contains water;
- closed containers while the stone is still damp.

Drying too quickly can create stress and cracks. Storing a wet stone in a box encourages mould and unpleasant odours.

A thick stone may feel dry on the surface while still containing moisture inside. Give it enough time before storage.

## Common mistakes

### Soaking every stone overnight

Some stones tolerate it; others are damaged. Follow the manufacturer.

### Using a fixed time without observing the stone

Five or ten minutes may be enough for one stone and too little or too much for another. Bubbles provide a useful visual guide for absorbent stones.

### Sharpening on a dry surface

The stone can load, drag and behave inconsistently.

### Ignoring flatness

A hollow stone changes the sharpening angle and makes results harder to repeat.

### Drying the stone with heat

Rapid drying can crack the stone.

### Storing the stone wet

Moisture trapped in a closed box can produce mould, smell and damage.

### Confusing slurry with the burr

Slurry is loose material on the stone. The burr is a thin lip of steel still attached to the blade edge.

## A practical preparation routine

1. Read the manufacturer's instructions.
2. Inspect the stone for cracks, dirt and uneven wear.
3. If it is an absorbent water stone, immerse it until bubbling substantially stops.
4. If it is splash-and-go, wet only the surface as directed.
5. Secure the stone on a stable non-slip base.
6. Check and correct flatness.
7. Wet the working surface.
8. Keep water available throughout sharpening.
9. Rinse, flatten if needed and air-dry after use.

Preparation should follow the **actual construction of the stone**, not a fixed ritual. Porous water stones need enough water in their pores to remain stable in use; dense and special stones may require little or no soaking. In every case the stone should be flat, secure and correctly lubricated before the edge touches it. Good preparation gives clearer feedback, more consistent abrasion and safer sharpening.
