# Preparing the Stone and Workspace

Good sharpening begins with a stable, flat and correctly wetted stone. Careful preparation makes the movement easier to control and prevents many mistakes that beginners wrongly blame on their technique.

## A simple first setup

For an ordinary kitchen knife that has become dull through normal use, one good medium-grit water stone is usually enough. A stone around 800-1500 grit can rebuild a practical kitchen edge without adding unnecessary steps.

A coarse stone is useful for chips, a very rounded edge or a deliberate change of geometry. A finer stone can make the finished edge smoother, but it is optional. A fine stone cannot compensate for an edge that was never properly sharpened on the first stone.

You also need:

- a non-slip stone holder or a damp cloth;
- water or the lubricant required by the stone manufacturer;
- a clean cloth;
- good light;
- enough clear space to move the complete blade over the stone.

## Prepare the stone correctly

Water stones do not all work in the same way. Some absorb water and need to soak. Others need only water on the surface and are often called **splash-and-go** stones. Diamond plates do not soak, and oil stones use oil rather than water.

Follow the manufacturer's instructions whenever they are available. For a traditional absorbent water stone, soak it until the stream of air bubbles has almost stopped. Do not leave an unknown stone submerged for hours: long soaking can damage some binders.

Keep the working surface wet during sharpening. Add water when the blade begins to drag, the sound becomes dry or dark residue stops moving freely. The grey mixture on the stone contains water, abrasive and removed steel. It is not the burr, which remains attached to the cutting edge.

## Check that the stone is flat

A stone wears most where the knife passes most often. With use, its centre can become hollow. A hollow surface changes the contact angle during the stroke and makes a consistent result much harder.

Place a straight edge across the stone in several directions and look for gaps. Pencil lines drawn across the surface are another useful check: during flattening, high areas lose the marks first and low areas keep them.

Flatten the stone with a suitable plate or lapping stone when necessary. A beginner does not need microscopic perfection, but the surface should form one reliable plane.

## Make the workspace stable

Place the stone on a holder, rubber base or folded damp cloth. Push it gently before beginning. It should not slide or rock.

Stand or sit at a height that lets you relax your shoulders and move your arms freely. Leave space for the knife to travel across the stone from tip to heel. Keep the area free from objects that could stop the movement.

The hand holding the handle controls direction and angle. The other hand rests lightly on the blade and applies pressure near the part of the edge that is crossing the stone. Keep the fingers above the blade face and away from the cutting edge.

## After sharpening

Rinse the knife and dry it immediately. Rinse the stone, remove loose residue and check whether it needs light flattening.

Let a water stone dry slowly in a ventilated place. Avoid radiators, ovens, strong sunlight and closed containers while the stone is still damp. Store it only when it is completely dry.

The preparation target is simple: a stone that is suitable for the work, correctly wetted, reasonably flat and completely stable.
