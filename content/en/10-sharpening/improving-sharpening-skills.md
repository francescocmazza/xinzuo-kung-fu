# Improving Your Sharpening Skills

The first chapter gives one dependable answer to most beginner problems: use the existing bevel, cover the complete edge, form a light burr on both sides, then finish with very light alternating passes.

Keep that method whenever it gives a good result. Improvement does not mean replacing a simple method with a complicated one. It means noticing more, correcting less and understanding when the simple method needs help.

Begin this chapter only after you can sharpen an ordinary double-bevel knife evenly from heel to tip. If marker contact, burr detection or the three-part paper test is still uncertain, more advanced choices will hide the basic problem rather than solve it.

## The three skills behind better sharpening

An improving sharpener learns to control three things:

1. **Contact:** exactly where the stone touches the knife.
2. **Pressure:** how strongly it cuts and how pressure changes during the session.
3. **Purpose:** whether the work is restoring the apex, correcting the blade behind it or changing the final cutting feel.

These skills reduce unnecessary grinding. They also explain why a knife can have a keen edge and still perform poorly in food.

## Read the evidence after every stage

The stone leaves visible and tactile evidence:

- marker removal shows contact;
- fresh scratches show where steel was removed;
- reflected light reveals remaining flat or damaged areas;
- the burr confirms that the abrasive reached the apex;
- resistance in paper maps uneven sections;
- resistance in food reveals both the edge and the blade geometry behind it.

Do not collect all this information at the end. Stop, rinse and inspect during the work. Early inspection turns a correction into a few controlled passes. Late inspection often discovers that too much steel has already been removed.

### Practice drill: predict before looking

Colour the bevel of a practice knife. Make one light pass while deliberately holding the spine slightly too low. Before inspecting it, predict where the ink should remain. Then repeat with the spine slightly too high.

This safe marker exercise teaches the connection between hand position and contact. Return to the correct angle and erase the intended strip evenly before sharpening further.

Pass the drill when your prediction matches the mark three times in a row.

## Build three pressure levels

Pressure has different jobs during one sharpening session.

### Working pressure

Use controlled pressure to remove steel and reach the apex. The stone should cut steadily without stopping the blade, moving the holder or making the wrist collapse.

### Refining pressure

Once the burr is continuous, use about half the previous pressure. The goal is now to replace coarse scratches and reduce the burr, not to keep bending it.

### Finishing pressure

The final alternating passes use little more than the weight of the knife and guiding fingers. They clean and centre the apex.

A beginner often applies one pressure from start to finish. The result may feel sharp while still carrying a wire burr. The clearest sign of progress is not faster movement; it is a visible reduction in pressure as the edge nears completion.

### Practice drill: make the burr smaller

Sharpen two similar inexpensive knives on the same medium stone. On the first, stop at the earliest continuous burr. On the second, continue for twenty unnecessary firm passes.

Deburr both with the same light sequence. Compare how long the process takes and whether the second burr keeps folding from side to side. The extra work usually makes the finish harder rather than better.

The lesson is practical: the smallest burr that confirms complete contact is enough.

## Introduce section work carefully

The complete-edge pass remains the safest default. Section work becomes useful when one area needs more attention, the blade is longer than the stone, or a local defect cannot be corrected efficiently with full passes.

Divide the edge into overlapping zones: heel, middle and front. Work one zone with short controlled strokes while keeping pressure directly above it. Overlap the neighbouring zone so no abrupt step develops. Finish with several light complete-edge passes to blend the whole edge.

Section work saves steel only when the sharpener can preserve the profile. Repeated strokes in one exact spot can create a hollow in the cutting line. Mark the bevel, inspect often and stop local work as soon as the missing contact or burr appears.

### Workshop example: one dull centimetre near the heel

The complete-edge beginner method would sharpen the whole knife until the heel improves. That is safe and may be the correct choice for a new sharpener.

An improving sharpener can make three to five short strokes over the heel, with the supporting fingers above it, then return to light full-edge passes. The correction ends when the burr and paper response match the rest of the blade.

The advanced method is not automatically better. It is better only when the user can keep the correction local without changing the profile.

### Section-work test

Colour the full bevel and leave one small area with extra marker. Work that area briefly, blend with full passes and inspect the edge line from the side.

Pass when the target mark is removed, the neighbouring bevel remains even and the paper test has no catch at either transition.

## Choose stones by the work

Grit numbers describe abrasive size, but they do not tell the whole story. Stone hardness, binder, abrasive type, cutting speed and slurry change how a stone behaves.

Use a simple three-stage decision:

- **Repair:** a coarse stone, usually below about 500 grit, for chips, profile correction or substantial thinning.
- **Sharpen:** a medium stone around 700-1500 grit for most dull edges.
- **Refine:** a finer stone around 2000-6000 grit when the task benefits from a smoother finish.

The ranges overlap between brands. Begin with the finest stone that can complete the required work in a reasonable time. A coarse stone saves time on damage but removes steel quickly. A fine stone gives control but cannot efficiently rebuild a badly rounded edge.

The abrasive must also cut the steel. Conventional water stones suit many common Xinzuo steels. Highly wear-resistant steels such as ZDP-189, SRS13, M390 and 14Cr14MoVNb may respond more efficiently to suitable ceramic or diamond abrasive. If the stone barely changes the bevel despite correct contact, improve the abrasive before increasing force.

### Hard and soft stones

A hard stone stays flat longer and shows contact clearly. This makes it useful for diagnosis and precise bevels. A softer stone releases abrasive readily and can feel smooth, but it dishes faster and can round a surface under heavy pressure.

Neither is a quality rank. Choose the behaviour that helps the stage you are performing.

### Water, slurry and a readable surface

The grey mixture on a water stone contains removed steel and loose abrasive. It is called slurry or swarf depending on what it contains. It is not the burr.

Rinse the surface when you need to see exact contact, when the stone feels sticky or when metal begins to clog it. Keep a controlled slurry when the stone cuts and finishes better with it. A beginner may simply keep the surface wet and clean; deliberate slurry management belongs at this second level because the user can now notice the difference.

Clean the knife, hands and holder before moving to a fine stone. One coarse particle can leave a deep scratch across a polished bevel.

## Keep the stone flat

The blade copies the surface beneath it. A hollow stone changes the angle during a stroke and makes accurate work harder.

Draw a pencil grid across the stone. Flatten until every line disappears. Check more often when using a soft stone, working a wide bevel or repeatedly sharpening the centre. Light, frequent flattening is faster than repairing a deeply dished stone.

### Flatness test

Check the stone with pencil before a session, after sharpening two knives and again after flattening. Pass when you can recognise the developing wear pattern and correct it before the hollow becomes obvious under a straightedge.

## Distinguish apex work from geometry work

Routine sharpening restores the narrow edge bevel. It brings the two sides together at the apex.

As sharpening repeats, that narrow bevel moves upward into thicker steel and becomes wider. The knife may cut paper well but wedge in a carrot or onion. The apex is sharp; the blade behind it is too thick.

**Thinning** removes steel behind the edge to restore easier passage through food. It is a separate operation from sharpening and removes much more material.

### A practical geometry comparison

Imagine two pencils sharpened to equally fine points. One tapers gradually; the other becomes thick immediately behind the point. Both points can pierce paper, but the gradual pencil passes through a narrow opening more easily.

A kitchen knife behaves in the same way. Paper mostly tests the point of the system. Firm food also tests the shape behind it.

### Recognise when thinning may help

Possible signs include:

- the knife passes paper but splits or wedges in firm vegetables;
- the edge bevel has become much wider after repeated sharpening;
- cutting resistance has increased even after a clean deburring;
- more force is needed once the apex has already entered the food.

Before thinning, inspect the blade finish. Damascus pattern, mirror polish, coatings, hammering and black forge scale can be scratched or removed. Protect valuable finishes and use professional service when the original grind is uncertain.

For a simple practice knife, colour the area immediately behind the edge. Lay the blade at a lower angle than the sharpening angle and abrade that shoulder gradually. Keep the cutting profile unchanged. Then recreate the narrow edge bevel and finish normally.

Do not try to make the visible bevel equally wide at heel and tip. Blade thickness usually changes toward the tip. Equal appearance can require unequal geometry.

### Geometry test

Compare the knife in printer paper and a firm carrot before and after normal sharpening. If paper improves but carrot resistance remains high, identify geometry as the likely next subject rather than moving automatically to a finer stone.

This test is passed when you can explain whether the problem begins at the apex or after the blade has entered the food. Actual thinning should wait until that distinction is clear.

## Choose the finish for the food

Higher grit is not automatically sharper in every useful sense. A medium finish leaves microscopic texture that can start easily in tomato skin, meat and fibrous food. A finer finish can feel smoother in push cuts and delicate slicing.

Use the same knife and compare two finishes over time. Finish it first on the medium stone, deburr carefully and test tomato, onion and paper. At the next genuine sharpening, add the finer stone and repeat the same tests.

Keep the finish that suits the knife's work. Prestige, mirror appearance and grit number matter less than controlled cutting.

## Improve deburring before adding polish

A wire edge can imitate excellent sharpness. It bends or breaks during the first cuts, so the knife seems to become dull immediately.

Prevent it by stopping at a small burr and reducing pressure early. Alternate sides more frequently near the end. Edge-leading strokes, where the edge moves into the abrasive, can cut weakened burr steel away efficiently. Edge-trailing strokes, where the spine leads, feel gentle but can draw out a new wire if pressure is high.

A clean leather or fabric strop is optional. Move spine first, use very few passes and press lightly. A soft strop under heavy force rounds the apex. The stone must do the main deburring; the strop is a final check, not a rescue tool.

### Stability test

Cut paper, make twenty normal cuts through cardboard-free food such as onion or carrot, clean the knife and test paper again. Pass when the edge feels substantially the same and no section develops a sudden catch.

The test is not about long-term edge retention. It checks whether the first result depended on a weak burr.

## Approach asymmetry with evidence

Some double-bevel knives have one side sharpened more than the other. Previous owners may also have created accidental asymmetry.

Do not equalise the two sides by counting identical strokes. Use marker, bevel width, steering in food and burr movement. Preserve a deliberate asymmetry when the knife cuts straight and the maker specifies it. Correct an accidental one gradually rather than trying to rebuild it in a single session.

If the knife always steers to one side in a tall food such as potato, first confirm that the cut and grip are straight. Then compare bevel angles, thickness and remaining burr. Steering is evidence, not a command to grind one side heavily.

## Traditional single-bevel knives

A yanagiba, deba or usuba needs a different method. Its broad front bevel works with a hollow reverse called the **urasuki**. Narrow flat lands around that hollow support the edge; light work on them is called **uraoshi**.

Do not apply the beginner's double-bevel method to these knives. The reverse is not sharpened at a normal raised angle. It is placed almost flat on a truly flat stone and touched with very light pressure. Raising it can create a secondary bevel and damage the geometry.

The broad front bevel may also change contact from heel to tip because blade height, thickness and twist vary. A hard medium-fine stone and marker can reveal high and low areas before coarse work begins.

For an improving sharpener, the safe first lesson is diagnosis:

1. flatten the stone carefully;
2. place the reverse flat and make a few nearly weightless passes;
3. confirm that contact stays on the narrow perimeter lands rather than inside the hollow;
4. place the broad front bevel on the stone and inspect its contact pattern;
5. stop if the blade rocks, appears bent or would need extensive coarse correction.

Routine refinement of a sound single bevel can be learned with model-specific instruction. Correcting the reverse hollow, wide lands, serious twist or a damaged ridge line belongs to an experienced specialist.

### Hamaguri in plain language

**Hamaguri** is a controlled convex transition on the front bevel. It can add support near an impact-prone edge, as on some deba sharpening strategies. It is not one compulsory shape for every Japanese knife.

Soft stones, thick slurry and rocking pressure can create accidental convexity. Learn flat contact first. A deliberate small microbevel is easier to control than vaguely rounding the whole surface.

## A five-session improvement plan

### Session 1: contact

Use a practice knife and marker. Predict the ink pattern, make one light pass and inspect. Repeat until the hand can reproduce the same narrow contact strip.

### Session 2: pressure

Sharpen normally, but name each stage aloud or in notes: working, refining, finishing. The final passes must be visibly lighter than the first.

### Session 3: burr control

Check after every group of five passes. Stop at the first continuous burr and record how little work was actually needed. Test stability after deburring.

### Session 4: local correction

Use marker to identify one incomplete section. Apply a few overlapping local strokes, then blend with complete-edge passes. Confirm the edge profile and paper response.

### Session 5: task finish

Compare a medium and a fine finish on the same type of food. Describe bite, smoothness, resistance and control without using the grit number as the conclusion.

## The second-level test

You have moved beyond beginner technique when you can demonstrate all of the following:

- predict and confirm contact with marker and scratches;
- use clearly different working, refining and finishing pressures;
- stop at a small continuous burr and produce a stable edge;
- correct one section without creating a hollow or transition catch;
- keep the stone flat and free from coarse contamination;
- distinguish a dull apex from excessive thickness behind the edge;
- choose a medium or fine finish by food performance;
- recognise a single-bevel knife and avoid damaging its reverse geometry.

Passing this test does not mean every repair should be attempted. It means you can read what the knife and stone are showing, choose a proportionate action and stop before uncertainty becomes damage.

## What expert practice adds

Expert sharpening uses the same evidence with greater precision. The sharpener may change stroke direction to read scratches, select a hard stone to diagnose contact, use soft-stone slurry to blend a bevel, minimise burr formation or vary support along a twisted single bevel.

Those techniques are valuable because the foundation is stable. Without angle control, pressure control and reliable testing, extra choices only create extra ways to be inconsistent.

Keep the complete-edge method as your safe home position. Depart from it for a clear reason, make the smallest correction that evidence supports, then return to full light passes and test the whole knife.
