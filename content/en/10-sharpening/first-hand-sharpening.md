# Your First Successful Hand Sharpening

The aim of a first sharpening session is simple: make the whole edge cut better from heel to tip.

You do not need a perfect angle, an expensive collection of stones or fast movements. You need one stable stone, one repeatable motion and clear signs that tell you what to do next.

This chapter deliberately teaches a forgiving method. Every normal pass covers the complete edge, from tip to heel. An experienced sharpener may work only on the area that needs correction, but a beginner can easily create sharp and dull zones that way. Removing a tiny amount of extra steel is a reasonable price for a first edge that works evenly.

Use this method on an ordinary double-bevel kitchen knife: a chef's knife, santoku, utility knife, nakiri or paring knife sharpened on both sides. Practise on a sound, inexpensive knife before using your most valuable one.

Do not use this method on:

- a serrated knife;
- a traditional single-bevel yanagiba, deba or usuba;
- a bent or twisted blade;
- a blade with a crack, a deep chip or a badly broken tip;
- a knife whose handle or bolster prevents the heel from reaching the stone.

These cases need a different technique or a repair before normal sharpening.

## What you are actually sharpening

Three parts are easy to confuse:

- The **primary bevel** is the broad surface that makes the blade thinner toward the edge.
- The **microbevel** is the very narrow terminal facet immediately above the cutting edge.
- The **apex** is the line where the two microbevels meet. This is the part that cuts.

In this beginner method, sharpen only the **microbevel**. Do not lay the broad primary bevel on the stone. Grinding the primary bevel changes the thickness and geometry of the blade. That operation is called thinning and belongs to the next chapter.

A dull apex has become rounded, bent or worn. The stone removes steel from the two microbevels until they meet again in a clean line.

When the stone reaches the apex from one side, it often pushes a tiny lip of steel toward the other side. This lip is the **burr**. A light burr along the entire edge tells a beginner that the stone has reached the apex everywhere.

The burr is a progress signal, not the finished edge. It must be removed before the knife is ready.

## What you need

Keep the first setup small:

- one medium water stone, ideally around 800-1200 grit;
- a stone holder, rubber base or damp folded cloth;
- water and a towel;
- a dark permanent marker;
- a pencil for checking stone flatness;
- ordinary printer paper for testing.

A 1000-grit stone is the simplest starting point for a normally dull kitchen knife. It removes steel quickly enough to reach the apex while remaining controllable.

A very coarse stone removes mistakes as quickly as it removes damage. Keep stones below about 500 grit for chips, profile repair and later training. A very fine stone can polish the microbevel without reaching a rounded apex efficiently. It is optional during the first sessions.

If the knife remains unchanged after many correct passes on a medium stone, stop before pressing harder. The knife may need a more effective abrasive, a coarser stone or a repair diagnosis. The next chapter explains those choices.

## Inspect the knife before sharpening

Clean and dry the blade. Look straight along the cutting line and then tilt the apex slowly under a strong light.

Check for:

- bright points or short bright lines along the apex;
- chips or dents;
- a rounded or damaged tip;
- a bend, twist or local hollow in the cutting line;
- corrosion close to the edge;
- a microbevel that is much wider on one side;
- a heel that cannot lie on the stone because the handle or bolster blocks it.

Small reflections normally show dull or rolled areas. Deep chips, cracks, obvious bends and severe profile damage are stop signs for the beginner method.

Make a slow paper cut with the heel, middle and tip separately. Remember or write down where the knife catches. This is your starting map. It lets you compare the same areas after sharpening instead of relying on memory.

## Prepare the stone and your workspace

Read the instructions supplied with the stone. Some water stones need soaking; others need only water on the surface. Soak or wet the stone exactly as its maker directs. Do not assume that every water stone can remain immersed. When an absorbent stone does require soaking, the slowing of air bubbles is a useful sign that its pores have filled with water; the maker's instructions still take priority.

Place the stone lengthwise on a stable support. Push gently on each corner. If it rocks or slides, fix the support before touching the knife to it.

The surface must be reasonably flat. Draw a loose pencil grid across the stone. Rub it against the recommended flattening plate until the lines disappear together. If the centre retains pencil while the ends become clean, the stone is hollow and needs more flattening.

Keep the stone wet during sharpening. Add water when the blade starts to drag, the sound becomes dry or the surface begins to feel sticky. The grey water is normal. It contains loose abrasive and steel removed from the knife; it is not the burr.

Stand or sit so your shoulders remain relaxed and your elbows can move freely. The stone should be high enough that you do not bend deeply over it, but low enough that your shoulders do not rise. Leave enough empty space to complete the whole pass without hitting the sink, wall or other objects.

## Find the microbevel with a marker

Look for the very narrow shiny strip immediately above the cutting edge. This is the microbevel. On some knives it is easy to see; on others it may be less than a millimetre wide.

Colour only the last one or two millimetres nearest the edge. Do not colour the broad primary bevel as if it were the surface to sharpen.

Rest the blade lightly on the stone. Raise the spine until the microbevel seems to meet the surface, then make one nearly weightless complete-edge pass. Inspect the ink.

- Ink removed above the microbevel but left at the apex means the spine was too low. The stone did not reach the cutting edge.
- Ink removed only from the extreme apex means the spine was higher than the existing microbevel angle.
- Ink removed across the microbevel and up to the apex means the contact is correct enough to begin.

The exact appearance does not need to be perfect. The important point is that the stone reaches the apex without scratching a wide band across the primary bevel.

Around 15 degrees per side is common for many kitchen knives, but it is not a universal specification. A useful visual approximation is to lift the spine by about one quarter of the blade height. For a 50 mm tall chef's knife, this is about 13 mm. A fixed stack of two coins cannot represent the same angle on knives of different heights and should not be used as a universal gauge.

Use the height estimate only to understand the scale of the movement. The marker tells you more because it reads the microbevel that is already on the knife.

## Learn one complete-edge pass

Hold the handle with your dominant hand. Place two or three fingertips from the other hand on the flat of the blade, safely behind the cutting edge. For the beginner method, keep them near the centre of the blade.

The fingertips rest on the blade and move together with it. Do not slide them from tip to heel while the knife is moving. Local finger pressure will be introduced only after your full-edge movement is reliable.

Start with the tip on the far part of the stone. Because the edge curves upward near the tip, begin with the handle already raised slightly. Draw the knife diagonally across the stone so the contact travels through this sequence:

> tip - curved front - middle - heel.

As contact moves from the tip through the curved front, lower the handle gradually. Keep its height almost unchanged as the straighter middle and heel cross the stone. At the end, remove pressure, lift the knife, return to the starting position and repeat.

During the working movement, let the cutting edge travel ahead of the spine, as if trying to slice an impossibly thin layer from the stone. This is called an edge-leading pass. Keep the angle stable and do not let the edge dig into the surface.

Do not grind during the return movement in the beginner method. One deliberate edge-leading movement followed by one unloaded return is easier to control than rapid movement in both directions.

Every normal working pass must include the entire edge. Use the same path each time. Move slowly enough to notice sound, resistance and contact. Speed hides mistakes and adds nothing to the result.

### How much pressure to use

Use three simple pressure levels:

1. **Working pressure:** enough to hear and feel the stone cutting, but not enough to bend the blade, move the stone, whiten the fingertips or tighten the shoulders.
2. **Refining pressure:** about half the working pressure once the burr is continuous.
3. **Finishing pressure:** little more than the weight of the knife and the hands guiding it.

If the knife sticks, shakes or changes angle, pressure is already too high. Add water, relax and slow down before trying again.

## The complete beginner method

### Step 1: make the starting map

Inspect the clean edge under light and test heel, middle and tip on printer paper. Do not sharpen a cracked, bent, deeply chipped or unusual blade.

### Step 2: mark the first microbevel

Colour only the narrow microbevel. Make one light complete-edge pass and adjust the spine until the ink disappears from the microbevel up to the apex from tip to heel.

Do not continue if the stone is scratching the broad primary bevel. Recolour the last one or two millimetres and repeat the contact check.

### Step 3: sharpen the first side

Make five slow complete-edge passes. Start with the handle slightly raised at the tip, lower it through the curve and finish at the heel. Keep the same microbevel angle and use controlled working pressure.

Rinse the edge and check for a burr on the side opposite the one you sharpened. Check at the heel, middle, curved front and tip.

If the burr is missing anywhere, make another two or three complete-edge passes. Continue to cover the whole edge, but pay closer attention as the missing area crosses the stone. Inspect again after each small group.

Stop as soon as the first light burr is continuous. Do not make it larger for reassurance.

### Step 4: sharpen the second side

Turn the knife over while keeping the handle in your dominant hand. Place the other fingertips safely on the flat and repeat the same complete path from tip to heel.

Work first in a group of five and then in groups of two or three. Stop when the burr has moved to the first side along the complete edge.

The second side may need a different number of passes. Stroke counts create rhythm; the burr tells you whether the apex has been reached.

### Step 5: reduce and remove the burr

Keep the complete-edge movement, but reduce both pressure and repetition:

1. three light passes on the first side;
2. three light passes on the second;
3. two lighter passes on the first;
4. two lighter passes on the second;
5. six to ten nearly weightless passes, changing side after every pass.

Rinse and inspect. If a directional burr remains, do not return to heavy pressure. Continue with one nearly weightless pass per side and check again.

This countdown is a starting routine, not proof that the burr has disappeared. The checks below decide when the knife is finished.

### Step 6: use a finer stone only after the medium stone succeeds

The first goal is a stable edge from the 1000-grit stone. Do not move to a finer stone to hide an incomplete microbevel.

If the knife already passes the light and paper checks and you have a stone around 3000 grit, clean the knife, hands and holder. Make only alternating complete-edge passes with finishing pressure. Do not raise another noticeable burr.

If you are unsure what the fine stone changed, stop on the medium stone. A clean, well-deburred 1000-grit microbevel is an excellent everyday kitchen edge.

### Step 7: clean before testing

Wash and dry the knife. Remove abrasive residue from the handle, blade and work surface. Carbon steel should be dried immediately.

Rinse the stone and let it dry slowly in a ventilated place as its maker recommends. Do not use an oven, radiator, hair dryer or strong direct sun. Do not close a damp stone inside a box.

Test the knife only after it is clean.

## Understand and check the burr safely

The burr is a microscopic lip still attached to the apex. It bends toward the side opposite the one being sharpened. The burred side may feel slightly rough or hooked when approached from the blade face.

Never slide a finger along the cutting edge.

To check with a fingertip, hold the knife still and move the fingertip very lightly from the blade face toward the edge, perpendicular to it. Stop as soon as you reach the apex. Repeat at the heel, middle, curved front and tip.

If you do not want to place a fingertip near the apex, use a small piece of cotton, tissue or thin paper. A burr may catch loose fibres more readily from one side. Good light or magnification can also help.

Do not confuse the burr with:

- grey slurry or steel residue stuck to the blade;
- rough scratches that feel the same from both directions;
- a rolled section that was already present before sharpening;
- a burr found at only one point, which proves only that one point reached the apex.

A large burr is not better. It can fold from side to side, cut paper briefly and then collapse in the first real use. Treat the burr as a receipt, not a trophy: the smallest continuous one is enough.

## Three checks after sharpening

No single test proves that the whole knife is ready. Use all three.

### Check 1: reflected light

Hold the clean, dry apex under a strong lamp and tilt it slowly. A clean apex appears mostly dark. A bright point or short bright line often marks a flat, rolled or chipped area.

One tiny reflection does not justify grinding the whole knife again. Note its position and compare it with the paper test.

### Check 2: paper in three sections

Use ordinary dry printer paper. Hold it so your supporting hand remains well away from the cutting path.

Test three overlapping sections separately:

1. heel to middle;
2. middle to curved front;
3. curved front to tip.

Cut slowly instead of slashing. Notice how much force starts the cut, whether resistance changes and whether the paper is sliced cleanly or torn. The three parts should behave similarly. A catch in the same position twice identifies an area that needs attention.

Paper alone is not enough. A wire burr can cut it once and then fold.

### Check 3: food and stability

A tomato checks whether the apex starts on smooth skin without sliding. An onion checks entry and movement through layers. A carrot also reveals thickness behind the microbevel, but that subject belongs to the next chapter.

Use the knife normally. Do not force it into food to prove that it is sharp.

After about twenty normal cuts through onion or carrot, clean the knife and repeat the three-part paper test. If the result becomes much worse immediately, a wire burr probably remained. Return only to the nearly weightless alternating passes, clean the knife and test again.

## Workshop examples

### The middle cuts, but the tip catches paper

The user held a useful angle on the straight part but did not begin with the handle raised enough to follow the tip.

Recolour the tip microbevel. Make three complete-edge passes per side. Start with the handle slightly raised, then lower it gradually through the curve. Test the tip again.

The beginner still covers the whole edge. Only the attention given to the tip changes.

### The knife cuts paper once, then feels dull

A flexible wire burr probably remained. More heavy sharpening would make it larger.

Return to the medium stone and use six to ten nearly weightless alternating complete-edge passes. Clean the knife, make several normal food cuts and test again.

### The marker disappears above the microbevel but remains at the edge

The angle is too low. The stone is polishing above the microbevel without reaching the apex.

Raise the spine slightly, recolour the last one or two millimetres and repeat one light contact pass.

### The marker disappears only from the very edge

The spine is higher than the existing microbevel angle. This can create a steeper new microbevel instead of following the one already present.

Lower the spine slightly and repeat the marker check. Stop adjusting when the stone reaches the complete microbevel and apex.

### The burr appears at the heel and middle but not at the tip

The tip has not reached the apex. Continue with two or three complete passes at a time. Begin with the handle slightly raised and watch the marker at the curved front.

Do not grind only the middle because its burr feels reassuring.

### One side forms a burr quickly and the other takes longer

This is common on a previously uneven microbevel. Do not force equal stroke counts. Complete the burr on each side and then use the same light alternating finish.

### The blade sticks and the movement becomes jerky

The stone may be dry or clogged, or the pressure may be too high. Add water, rinse the surface if needed and reduce pressure. A slow smooth pass sharpens better than a forceful pass that changes angle.

### The knife cuts paper but wedges in a carrot

The microbevel may be sharp while the blade behind it is too thick. Repeated sharpening of the microbevel will not solve that resistance. This is a geometry problem and introduces thinning in the next chapter.

## Mistakes that matter most

### Sharpening the primary bevel instead of the microbevel

The primary bevel is the broad surface above the narrow terminal facet. Grinding it changes blade geometry and can damage a polished, hammered or Damascus finish. The beginner method touches only the microbevel.

### Changing the angle during every pass

An approximate angle repeated well works better than a perfect angle touched only occasionally. Slow down and repeat the marker check.

### Pressing harder when nothing happens

First check microbevel contact, water and stone condition. Pressure cannot compensate for missing the apex.

### Stopping the pass in the middle

The complete-edge method works because every normal pass includes tip, middle and heel. Remove pressure, reset and repeat the full path.

### Chasing a large burr

A large burr feels obvious but is harder to remove. Stop when the first light burr is continuous.

### Testing only the middle

The middle is usually easiest to sharpen. Test the heel, curved front and tip separately as well.

### Starting on a damaged or expensive knife

Large chips, unusual geometry and valuable finishes create problems that a first session does not need. Learn the movement on a simple knife first.

## Base-level exercises

These exercises turn a lucky result into a repeatable skill.

### Exercise 1: stable setup

Prepare the stone, then push gently on each corner and make ten unloaded practice movements. Pass when the stone stays still, the shoulders remain relaxed and the movement finishes without hitting an obstacle.

### Exercise 2: the marker line

Colour only the microbevel. Make three very light complete-edge passes per side. Pass when ink is removed from the microbevel up to the apex at the heel, middle and tip without creating a wide scratched strip across the primary bevel.

If the result varies, recolour and repeat more slowly. Do not continue to burr formation until contact is understandable.

### Exercise 3: the burr map

Sharpen one side in small groups and check heel, middle, curved front and tip after every group. Pass when you can identify the first light continuous burr and stop without making it much larger.

### Exercise 4: pressure reduction

Say or note the three stages while working: working, refining and finishing. Pass when the final alternating movements are clearly lighter than the first passes and the knife no longer carries a directional burr.

### Exercise 5: the three-part paper test

Test heel-middle, middle-front and front-tip. Pass when all three sections start and travel with similar resistance and none repeatedly tears the paper.

### Exercise 6: edge stability

Make about twenty normal cuts in onion or carrot, clean the knife and repeat the paper test. Pass when the edge behaves substantially as it did before the food test.

### Exercise 7: repeat the result

Sharpen a second ordinary knife, or repeat the process after the first knife has genuinely become dull again. Pass when the result is similar without having to rediscover the movement from the beginning.

## Your base-level test

Move to the next chapter only after you can answer yes to every statement in two different sharpening sessions:

- I can identify a suitable double-bevel practice knife and recognise the cases that require me to stop.
- I can prepare a flat, wet and stable stone without it moving.
- I can distinguish the primary bevel, microbevel and apex.
- I can use marker to place only the microbevel on the stone from tip to heel.
- Every normal working pass covers the complete edge.
- I begin with the handle slightly raised at the tip and lower it through the curve.
- I can form the smallest useful continuous burr and stop.
- I can reduce pressure and remove the directional burr with alternating passes.
- Heel, middle and tip pass the paper test with similar resistance.
- The result remains stable after normal food cutting.

Passing once can be luck. Passing in two sessions shows that the method is repeatable.

At this point you have learned the essential skill: controlled contact on the microbevel along the complete edge. The next chapter teaches when and how to depart from this safe beginner method.
