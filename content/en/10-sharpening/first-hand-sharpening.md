# Your First Successful Hand Sharpening

The aim of a first sharpening session is simple: make the whole edge cut better from heel to tip.

You do not need a perfect angle, an expensive collection of stones or fast movements. You need one stable stone, one repeatable motion and a way to check your result.

This chapter deliberately teaches a forgiving method. Every normal pass covers the complete edge, from tip to heel. An experienced sharpener may work only on the area that needs correction, but a beginner can easily create sharp and dull zones that way. Removing a tiny amount of extra steel is a reasonable price for a first edge that works evenly.

Use this method on an ordinary double-bevel kitchen knife: a chef's knife, santoku, utility knife, nakiri or paring knife sharpened on both sides. Do not use it on a serrated knife or a traditional single-bevel yanagiba, deba or usuba. Deep chips, a bent blade or a badly broken tip also need more advanced work.

## What sharpening changes

The edge of a dull knife has become rounded or bent. The stone removes steel from both sides until they meet again in a narrow cutting line called the **apex**.

When the stone reaches that line from one side, it often pushes a tiny lip of steel toward the other side. This lip is the **burr**. Feeling a light burr along the entire edge tells a beginner that the stone has reached every part of it.

The burr is a progress signal. It must be removed before the knife is finished.

## What you need

Keep the first setup small:

- one medium water stone, ideally around 800-1200 grit;
- a stone holder, rubber base or damp folded cloth;
- water and a towel;
- a dark permanent marker;
- ordinary printer paper for the final test.

A 1000-grit stone is the simplest starting point for a normally dull kitchen knife. A combination stone with a finer second side is useful but optional. Very coarse stones remove metal quickly and turn small mistakes into large ones. Very fine stones polish well but work slowly on a dull edge.

Practise first on a sound, inexpensive knife. Avoid your most valuable knife until the movement feels natural. The practice knife should be dull but free from large chips and bends.

## Prepare the stone

Read the instructions supplied with the stone. Some water stones need soaking; others need only water on the surface. Soak or wet it exactly as directed.

Place the stone lengthwise in front of you and make sure it cannot slide. The top should be flat. If you are unsure, draw several pencil lines across it and use the recommended flattening plate until the lines disappear together.

Keep the stone wet during sharpening. Add water when the blade starts to drag or the surface begins to dry. The dark grey water is normal: it contains stone particles and steel removed from the knife.

## Find the angle with a marker

Look for the narrow shiny strip immediately above the cutting edge. This is the edge bevel you want to place on the stone.

Colour that strip with the marker. Rest the knife on the stone, then raise the spine slowly until the bevel seems flat against the surface. Make one very light pass and inspect the ink.

- Ink removed above the edge but left at the very edge means the spine was too low.
- Ink removed only from the very edge means the spine was too high.
- Ink removed across most of the narrow bevel means the angle is close enough.

Around 15 degrees per side is common for many kitchen knives. Two medium coins under the spine can give a beginner a rough picture of that height on a typical chef's knife, but coins are not a measuring tool. Blade height changes the result. The marker tells you more because it reads the actual knife.

Do not keep searching for a perfect number. Find an angle that follows the existing bevel and that you can repeat.

## Learn the complete-edge pass

Hold the handle with your main hand. Place two or three fingertips of the other hand lightly on the blade, close to the part touching the stone. Keep every fingertip well above the cutting edge.

Start with the tip on the far part of the stone. Draw the knife diagonally across the surface so the contact moves smoothly from the tip, through the curved front and middle, to the heel. At the end, lift the knife, return to the start and repeat.

One pass always covers the whole edge:

> tip - curved front - middle - heel.

Use the same path each time. Move slowly enough to notice where the knife touches. Speed hides mistakes and adds nothing to the result.

As the curved tip crosses the stone, raise the handle slightly so the edge remains in contact. The movement is small and gradual. Too much lift rounds the tip; no lift may leave it dull.

Move the supporting fingers with the contact area. They begin near the front and travel toward the middle and heel during the pass. Their job is to keep gentle, even contact, not to bend the blade against the stone.

### A useful pressure comparison

During the first stage, use about the pressure you would use to wipe a dried mark from a kitchen surface: firm enough to work, light enough to stay controlled. If the blade shakes, the stone moves or your shoulders tighten, reduce pressure.

During finishing, use the pressure of wiping away dust. The last passes should feel almost weightless.

## The beginner's complete method

### Step 1: inspect before starting

Look along the edge under a strong light. Small bright points show dull or damaged areas. Check that there are no deep chips, bends or cracks.

Cut a sheet of printer paper slowly with the heel, middle and tip. Remember where it catches. This gives you a fair before-and-after comparison.

### Step 2: mark the first bevel

Colour the narrow edge bevel. Make one light complete-edge pass and adjust the angle until the ink disappears evenly enough from tip to heel.

The word *enough* matters. A first session does not require a perfectly uniform polished strip. It requires the stone to reach the edge without grinding far above it.

### Step 3: sharpen the first side

Make five slow complete-edge passes. Keep the same angle and move the supporting fingers with the blade. Wet the stone when needed.

Clean the edge carefully and check for a burr on the side opposite the one you sharpened. Move a fingertip very lightly from the blade face toward the edge and stop as soon as you reach the apex; never move along the edge. Check near the heel, middle and tip.

If the burr is not continuous, make another group of five complete-edge passes. Keep covering the whole edge, but apply a little more attention as the missing area crosses the stone. Repeat until you can detect a light burr everywhere.

Do not try to make it large. The first continuous burr is enough.

### Step 4: sharpen the second side

Turn the knife over and mirror the same movement. Reset at the tip, travel through the full edge and finish at the heel on every pass.

Work in groups of five until the burr moves to the first side along the complete edge. The second side may need a different number of passes. The burr, not the count, tells you when the stone has reached the apex.

### Step 5: reduce the burr

Keep using the same complete-edge movement, but reduce both pressure and repetition:

1. five light passes on the first side;
2. five light passes on the second;
3. three lighter passes on the first;
4. three lighter passes on the second;
5. ten nearly weightless passes, changing side after every pass.

This simple countdown is not the only way to deburr. It is easy to remember and gives a beginner a clean transition from sharpening pressure to finishing pressure.

### Step 6: use the finer stone only if available

If you have a finer side or second stone around 3000 grit, clean the knife and repeat ten very light alternating complete-edge passes. Do not try to raise another large burr. The finer stone is refining an edge that already exists.

If you have only the 1000-grit stone, finish there. A well-deburred 1000-grit edge is excellent for everyday kitchen work.

### Step 7: clean and test

Wash and dry the knife. Rinse the stone and leave it to dry as its manufacturer recommends. Keep abrasive residue away from food.

Test the knife only after cleaning.

## Three simple tests

### Test 1: reflected light

Hold the edge under a strong lamp and tilt it slowly. A clean apex appears mostly dark. A bright point or short bright line often marks a flat, rolled or chipped area.

One tiny reflection does not justify restarting the whole process immediately. Note its position and compare it with the paper test.

### Test 2: paper in three sections

Use ordinary printer paper. Cut slowly and safely, away from the hand holding it.

Test the edge in three overlapping sections:

1. heel to middle;
2. middle to curved front;
3. curved front to tip.

The knife should enter without a hard push and travel without sudden catches. The cut does not have to be silent or decorative. You are checking whether all three parts behave similarly.

### Test 3: a tomato or onion

A tomato checks whether the edge begins a cut without sliding over the skin. An onion checks whether the blade enters the layers cleanly without excessive force.

Do not press the knife onto the food to prove it is sharp. Use the normal cutting movement. The edge should start more readily than it did before sharpening.

## Workshop examples

### The middle cuts, but the tip catches paper

This is the most common first result. The user held a good angle on the straight part but did not follow the curve.

Return to the medium stone. Make five complete-edge passes per side. As the tip crosses the stone, lift the handle slightly and keep the supporting fingers close to the front. Test again.

The beginner still covers the whole edge. The small change is where attention and gentle pressure follow the pass.

### The knife cuts paper once, then feels dull

A flexible wire burr probably remained. Return to the finishing sequence and use less pressure: three passes per side, then ten alternating nearly weightless passes. Clean and test again.

More heavy sharpening would make the burr larger. The correction is lighter contact.

### The marker disappears above the edge but the knife stays dull

The angle is too low. The stone is polishing the shoulder of the bevel without reaching the apex. Raise the spine slightly, recolour the bevel and repeat the marker test.

### One side forms a burr quickly and the other takes longer

This is normal on a previously uneven edge. Do not force equal stroke counts. Complete the burr on each side, then use the same light alternating finish.

### The blade sticks and the movement becomes jerky

Add water, check that the stone is clean and reduce pressure. A controlled slow pass sharpens better than a forceful pass that changes angle.

## Mistakes that matter most

### Changing the angle during every pass

An approximate angle repeated well works better than a perfect angle touched only occasionally. Slow down and use the marker again.

### Pressing harder when nothing happens

First check the angle, water and stone surface. Pressure cannot compensate for missing the edge.

### Stopping the pass in the middle

The complete-edge method works because every pass includes tip, middle and heel. Reset and repeat the full path.

### Chasing a large burr

A large burr feels reassuring but is harder to remove. Stop when the first light burr is continuous.

### Testing only the middle

The middle is usually easiest to sharpen. Always test three overlapping sections.

### Starting on a damaged or expensive knife

Large chips, unusual geometry and valuable finishes add problems that a first session does not need. Learn the movement on a simple knife first.

## Four exercises before moving on

These exercises turn a lucky result into a repeatable skill.

### Exercise 1: the marker line

Colour the complete bevel. Make five very light passes per side. Pass when the ink is removed from the intended narrow bevel along most of the edge without a wide scratched band above it.

If the result varies, practise only this exercise. Recolour and repeat with slower movement.

### Exercise 2: the burr map

Sharpen one side in groups of five and check heel, middle and tip after every group. Pass when you can identify the first light continuous burr and stop without making it much larger.

This exercise teaches observation, not speed.

### Exercise 3: the three-part paper test

Finish and deburr the knife. Test heel-middle, middle-front and front-tip. Pass when all three sections cut with similar resistance and none tears the paper repeatedly.

### Exercise 4: repeat the result

Sharpen a second ordinary knife, or repeat the process after the first knife has genuinely become dull again. Pass when the result is similar without needing to rediscover the movement from the beginning.

## Your readiness check

Move to the next chapter when you can answer yes to all five statements:

- I can secure and prepare the stone without it moving.
- I can use marker to find the existing edge bevel.
- Every normal pass travels from tip to heel.
- I can recognise a light continuous burr and remove it with lighter alternating passes.
- Heel, middle and tip pass the paper test with similar resistance.

At this point you have learned the essential skill: repeatable contact along the complete edge. The next chapter teaches when and how to depart from this safe beginner method.
