# A Practical Beginner Sharpening Method

Sharpening removes steel until the two sides of the cutting edge meet again in a clean apex. A beginner does not need speed, a large collection of stones or perfect knowledge of metallurgy. The essential skills are simpler:

- place the narrow **edge bevel** on the stone;
- keep the same local contact angle along the complete edge;
- let both the forward and return movement sharpen;
- work the two sides evenly;
- listen to how the contact changes;
- create and then remove a small continuous burr.

This method is intended for ordinary double-bevel kitchen knives. Traditional single-bevel knives need a different method and should be learned separately.

## 1. Inspect the knife

Look along the cutting edge under strong light. Dull, rolled or chipped areas often reflect light. Check the tip, curved front, middle and heel.

A normally dull knife can usually be sharpened on one medium stone. A chipped edge, a broken tip or a knife that has become very thick behind the edge needs repair or geometry work. Those operations remove much more steel and are outside this beginner method.

## 2. Find the edge bevel

A kitchen knife has several different surfaces near the edge. The broad primary bevel makes the blade thinner toward the cutting edge. The **edge bevel** is the much narrower facet immediately beside the apex. When this facet is especially narrow and deliberately distinct from the geometry behind it, it is often called a **microbevel**.

Routine sharpening of an ordinary double-bevel knife usually works on this narrow edge bevel. Laying the whole broad primary bevel on the stone changes the blade geometry and removes much more steel than necessary.

A marker is the easiest learning aid. Colour only the narrow edge bevel and make a few light movements on the stone. Then inspect the ink:

- ink left at the apex means the angle is too low;
- contact only on the extreme edge means the angle is steeper than the existing edge bevel;
- ink removed evenly across the narrow facet means the contact is close to correct.

As a rough visual reference, raise the spine until the gap between the spine and the stone is about one quarter of the blade's height. This corresponds to roughly 15 degrees per side on a simple flat approximation. It is only a starting reference: following the existing edge bevel is more useful than forcing every knife to one number.

## 3. Position the hands

Hold the handle firmly but without tension. The handle hand controls the knife's direction and contact angle. Rest the fingers of the other hand on the blade face, safely above the edge, near the section that is crossing the stone.

Keep the shoulders relaxed and use the arms and upper body for the movement. A rigid wrist and excessive grip make the angle harder to control.

In the method taught in this book, when you turn the knife to sharpen the second side, also change the hand that holds the handle. This gives the second face a movement that mirrors the first and can make it easier for a beginner to preserve the same contact angle. Other experienced sharpeners may use different hand arrangements successfully; the important result is stable, controlled contact with the existing edge bevel.

Practise the movement slowly with almost no pressure until both hands can follow the full edge comfortably.

## 4. Use the complete stone and complete edge

Set the knife diagonally on the stone. Begin with the tip area in contact, then sweep the blade sideways so the working point travels through the curved front and middle to the heel. Reverse the movement and return along the same path.

Both the outward movement and the return movement can sharpen the knife when angle and pressure remain controlled. In this method, the return is used as an active sharpening stroke rather than only as a reset. This back-and-forth action makes the work faster and practical for a beginner.

Move the supporting fingers with the section being sharpened. Do not leave all the pressure in the middle of the blade. Use the full surface of the stone so that the stone and knife both wear more evenly.

## 5. Follow the changing blade profile

The cutting edge is rarely straight from heel to tip. Its curve changes the way each section meets the flat stone.

Adapt the grip and orientation of the knife continuously so that the **contact angle between the stone and the local edge bevel remains the same along the whole cutting edge**, despite changes in the blade's shape. Watch and feel the contact from tip through the curved section, middle and heel.

Think about preserving contact, not performing a separate gesture at the tip. There is no instruction to lift the handle by a predetermined amount. Each knife has a different profile, so the hands must follow the actual blade in front of them.

Use the marker again if the tip or heel remains untouched. It shows whether the movement follows the full edge bevel without making the front of the edge unnecessarily steep.

## 6. Listen to the stone

Sound is one of the most useful guides during sharpening.

At the beginning, contact often produces a strong scratching noise. The blade may also feel rough against the stone. Continue with a stable angle and controlled pressure. As the stone evens the edge bevel, the sound normally becomes smoother and the blade begins to glide more freely. A good, even contact can resemble the smooth sound of skis moving over snow.

Listen to the complete journey from tip to heel. A sudden harsh sound or change in resistance at one point may show that the angle changed, the stone is becoming dry or that one section needs more work.

Do not count a fixed number of passes. The required work changes with the knife, steel, stone and pressure. Instead, listen for the sound to move from scratching toward smooth sliding. When it no longer changes over several complete movements, the stone has usually worked the edge bevel evenly. Confirm this with the marker pattern and visual inspection.

Sound is feedback, not proof by itself. The burr confirms that the abrasive has reached the apex.

## 7. Progress evenly on both sides

During the first stage, change sides from time to time. In the method taught here, also change the handle hand. Give the two faces a broadly similar amount of work so that the edge develops evenly. The aim is balance, not an exact numerical count.

Check occasionally for a burr. If one forms almost immediately, inspect the angle before doing more work: an angle that is too steep can create a burr quickly at the cost of a wider and less efficient edge.

When the sound has become stable and the marker shows even contact, work one side until the smallest detectable burr runs continuously from heel to tip. Then change side and work the other face until the burr moves back along the complete edge.

If the burr is missing only in one area, concentrate briefly on that area and then return to complete passes. Avoid continuing heavy work on sections that have already reached the apex.

## 8. Reduce pressure and remove the burr

The main sharpening stage creates the new apex. The finishing stage cleans and stabilizes it.

Reduce pressure gradually. Alternate sides more frequently. If you are following the hand-switching method taught here, change the handle hand each time. Continue using the full outward and return movement, but let the blade become progressively lighter on the stone.

Finish with only enough pressure to keep the edge bevel in contact. Check that no rough directional hook remains on either side. A large or persistent burr usually responds better to lighter, more controlled passes than to further heavy grinding.

A strop can remove a very fine remaining burr, but it is optional. The stone should already have produced a clean and stable edge.

## 9. Decide whether a finer stone is useful

A medium finish is practical for most kitchen work and keeps useful bite on tomato skin, meat and fibrous vegetables. A finer stone can produce a smoother edge for delicate slicing or push cutting.

Move to a finer stone only after the first stone has reached the apex along the whole edge and the burr is under control. Use the same contact method with less pressure. A higher grit is a finishing choice, not proof of better sharpening.

## 10. Test the result

Rinse and dry the knife before testing it.

Do not use your fingers to test whether the knife is sharp. Finger contact at the edge is used only to detect a burr during sharpening, with the controlled perpendicular method described in [Understanding and Removing the Burr](the-burr.md). Test sharpness only by cutting suitable food or paper.

### Look at the edge

Inspect the apex under strong light. A clean sharp edge reflects very little. Bright points can reveal a remaining flat, roll or small chip.

### Check the burr

Check heel, middle, curved front and tip. Neither side should have a directional hook.

### Cut paper slowly

Hold a clean dry sheet with the supporting hand far from the cutting path. Start near the heel and draw the edge smoothly through the paper toward the tip. Do not make a fast, uncontrolled slash.

Notice where resistance changes. A catch or tear identifies an area that needs attention. On a long knife, test heel to middle and then middle to tip in two overlapping sections.

### Test on suitable food

A tomato shows whether the edge enters a resistant skin without pressure. An onion shows whether it passes cleanly through layers. Herbs reveal crushing. Choose a test that matches the knife's intended work.

Paper can be cut by a fragile wire edge, so combine the paper test with the burr check and real cutting behaviour.

## Practical advice that improves the result

### Use the abrasive instead of extra pressure

More pressure is rarely the answer to slow progress. Heavy pressure makes the angle unstable and creates a larger burr. If the stone hardly removes steel, it may be too fine, glazed or unsuitable for that steel.

### Keep the stone wet and stable

A dry or moving stone changes both sound and control. Add water when the surface drags and stop immediately if the support moves.

### Correct only the area that needs more work

If the tip, middle or heel has received less sharpening, give that area a short period of focused work. Then return to complete passes so the finished edge remains connected and even.

### Keep the burr small

The burr is evidence that the apex has been reached. It is not something to enlarge. Stop the heavy stage as soon as it is light and continuous.

### Accept slow, controlled movements

Speed does not improve the edge. A slow movement that maintains edge-bevel contact teaches more than a rapid movement with an unstable angle.

## Common beginner mistakes

- sharpening the broad primary bevel instead of the narrow edge bevel;
- fixing the hands in one position while the blade profile changes;
- wasting the return stroke when using the back-and-forth method taught here;
- changing side without first establishing a comfortable, controlled hand position;
- counting passes instead of listening and inspecting;
- treating an immediate burr as proof that the angle is correct;
- overworking the middle while missing the tip or heel;
- using heavy pressure during finishing;
- leaving the burr attached;
- moving to a fine stone before the first stone has reached the apex.

## Basic-level self-test

You are ready to practise more independently when you can answer **yes** to all of these questions:

1. Can you identify the edge bevel and confirm its contact with a marker?
2. Can you use both directions of the movement without losing the local angle?
3. Can you follow the entire edge while keeping continuous edge-bevel contact?
4. Can you change side and establish a stable hand position comfortably?
5. Can you hear the change from rough scratching to smoother sliding?
6. Can you keep the work broadly balanced without relying on a fixed pass count?
7. Can you detect a light burr safely at heel, middle, curve and tip?
8. Can you move the burr to the other side and then remove it with lighter alternating passes?
9. Can the finished edge cut consistently along its whole length without a remaining directional hook?

If one answer is no, repeat that specific part on an inexpensive practice knife. The purpose of the basic level is not speed or polish. It is complete, controlled and repeatable contact with the edge bevel.

## The complete routine in brief

1. Prepare a flat, wet and stable medium stone.
2. Inspect the edge and mark the edge bevel.
3. Set the local contact angle.
4. Sharpen in both directions across the complete edge using the controlled back-and-forth method taught here.
5. Adapt the grip continuously to preserve the same contact angle through every change in blade profile.
6. Listen as the sound changes from scratching to smooth sliding.
7. Alternate sides periodically, keeping the work broadly equal; if following this book's method, switch the handle hand as well.
8. Confirm a small continuous burr on one side, then move it from the other.
9. Reduce pressure, alternate sides and remove the burr.
10. Inspect, test, clean and dry the knife and stone.

The most important skill is learning to interpret the contact between the knife and the stone. The marker shows where the stone touches, the sound shows how that contact is changing and the burr confirms that the work has reached the apex.
