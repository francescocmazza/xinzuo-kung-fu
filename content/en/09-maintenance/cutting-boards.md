# Cutting Boards

A cutting board is not just a place to put food. It is the surface that the edge meets at the end of almost every cut. For that reason, the board is part of the cutting system: it affects edge life, cutting feel, food hygiene and, over time, the service life of the knife itself.

This matters most with very sharp knives. A thin edge enters food with little resistance, but that same thin cross-section is more sensitive to repeated impact against a surface that is too hard. A good knife on the wrong board can lose sharpness surprisingly quickly.

## Wood: our first choice for most kitchens

For normal home use, and whenever preserving the edge is a priority, **a well-made wooden cutting board is our preferred choice**.

Suitable hardwoods include **maple, beech, walnut and cherry**. The aim is not to find the hardest possible material. The board should resist wear while still yielding slightly when the edge reaches it. Very soft woods scar too easily; extremely hard or abrasive surfaces are less friendly to a fine edge.

Wood also should not automatically be treated as less hygienic than plastic. Classic studies found lower recoverable bacterial counts on several wooden surfaces than on plastic, while other food-service studies found plastic easier to clean and sanitize under standardized commercial procedures. A 2025 study comparing sugar maple with HDPE again found rapid reductions of *E. coli* on maple and generally higher recoverable counts on HDPE. The practical conclusion is simple: **material matters, but condition, cleaning, drying and replacement matter just as much**.

Wash a wooden board after use, do not leave it soaking in water, and let it dry completely. Deep splits, loose laminations and grooves that can no longer be cleaned properly are signs that the board should be resurfaced or replaced.

## End grain: why “wood of the head” is gentler on the edge

Among wooden boards, the construction we prefer for fine knives is **end grain**, often called **butcher-block end grain**. In Italian this can be described as *legno di testa*: the cut ends of the wood fibres face upward toward the working surface.

Imagine wood as a bundle of very small straws. In a conventional **edge-grain** board, the long fibres run mainly parallel to the surface, so the knife tends to cross them. In an **end-grain** board, the fibre ends point upward. The knife can therefore move partly **between** the fibres, which separate and flex slightly around the edge instead of presenting the same continuous crosswise resistance.

This is the reason good end-grain boards often show shallower-looking cut marks and are sometimes described as “self-healing.” The wood does not regenerate. The small cuts simply tend to close visually as the fibres return toward their previous position.

A good edge-grain board is still an excellent and usually less expensive choice. End grain becomes especially attractive when the knife has a very thin edge, the board is used heavily, and weight, cost and maintenance are not the main constraints.

## Plastic: practical hygiene, but a real microplastic trade-off

**Polyethylene (PE)** and **polypropylene (PP)** cutting boards are widely used in professional kitchens. Their advantages are obvious: they are inexpensive, non-porous, easy to standardize, available in colour-coded systems, and compatible with repeatable washing and sanitizing procedures. This operational convenience is one reason plastic remains common in food service.

The trade-off is mechanical wear. Every knife stroke that scores the surface can remove a microscopic amount of polymer.

A 2023 study in *Environmental Science & Technology* measured microplastic release from PE and PP cutting boards during chopping. Under the study's assumptions, the estimated annual exposure reached **tens of millions of particles per person**, with PP generally releasing more particles than PE in the tested conditions. The same study's preliminary 72-hour cell test did not find reduced viability from the tested PE particles, so the presence of microplastics must not be presented as proof of a demonstrated human health effect.

A 2025 mouse study went further by preparing diets on PE, PP and willow boards. Mice exposed for 12 weeks to food prepared on PP boards showed markers consistent with intestinal inflammation and impaired barrier function, while the PE group showed changes in gut microbiota and metabolism without the same evident inflammatory pattern. **This was an animal study, not evidence that a plastic cutting board causes the same effects in people.** It does, however, strengthen the case for taking avoidable microplastic exposure seriously while human risk is still being clarified.

For that reason, when there is no operational requirement that makes plastic preferable, **we recommend wood as the default choice and regard reducing plastic-board use as a reasonable precaution**. If plastic is used, replace boards once they become heavily scored or develop grooves that are difficult to clean.

## Bamboo: usable, but not our preferred surface for very fine edges

Bamboo is often sold beside wooden cutting boards, although botanically it is a grass rather than a tree. It has genuine practical advantages: it absorbs relatively little moisture, is hard, stable and resistant to scarring. USDA food-safety guidance accepts bamboo cutting boards and notes these characteristics.

For fine knife edges, however, those same properties are not necessarily ideal. Bamboo has a highly reinforced fibrous structure and can accumulate significant amounts of silica in its tissues. Silica contributes to the stiffness and wear resistance of many grasses, including bamboo. We therefore do **not** describe bamboo as unsafe or universally unsuitable; instead, **we simply do not recommend it as our first choice when the priority is maximizing the life of a very thin, highly sharpened edge**.

## Synthetic rubber and resilient professional boards

A useful alternative is the professional board made from **synthetic rubber or other resilient elastomers**, a category seen particularly often in Japanese professional kitchens. A good formulation is firm enough to give stable support while still yielding slightly under the edge.

From the knife's point of view, that is the right principle: a slightly resilient surface absorbs part of the contact instead of returning the whole load to the apex. High-quality rubber boards can therefore be very edge-friendly while also offering easier standardized cleaning than many wooden boards.

The limitation is that products in this category vary. Surface hardness, additives, temperature limits and manufacturer-approved cleaning methods are not identical, so the board should be judged by its actual specification rather than by the generic word “rubber.”

## Wood-fibre and resin composites

Boards made from compressed paper or wood fibres bonded with resin are thin, stable and resistant to water. Some are dishwasher-safe and extremely durable. They are convenient, but they are usually harder and less yielding than traditional wood.

We therefore place them **between wood and very hard surfaces** from the point of view of edge friendliness. They can be perfectly usable, especially with robust everyday knives, but we would choose a more yielding board for very thin, hard, finely sharpened blades.

## Glass, stone, ceramic and metal: presentation surfaces, not cutting surfaces

For the edge, the simplest rule in this chapter is also the most important:

**Do not use glass, marble, granite, ceramic, stone or metal as routine cutting surfaces.**

They can be attractive for serving or as worktop materials, but they yield very little when struck by the edge. The load is concentrated on a microscopic line of steel. A thicker, softer edge mainly loses sharpness quickly; a very thin, hard edge can also be more vulnerable to local deformation or microchipping when abused against an unforgiving surface.

The apparent paradox is useful to remember: **the sharper and thinner the knife, the more important the cutting board becomes.**

## The practical rule

When preserving the edge is the priority, choose a surface that gives slightly under the knife.

- **Best general choice:** a good wooden board; end grain when practical.
- **Excellent professional alternative:** a quality resilient synthetic-rubber board.
- **Plastic:** operationally practical and easy to sanitize, but subject to scoring and documented microplastic release.
- **Bamboo:** usable and food-safe, but not our first choice for the finest edges.
- **Fibre/resin composites:** convenient and durable, but usually harder than wood.
- **Glass, stone, ceramic and metal:** avoid as cutting surfaces.

A good cutting board does not make a knife sharper. It simply stops the surface underneath the food from undoing the work that created the edge.
